# 02. The Philosophy of Cybersecurity

**ISBN** 9781000956009  |  **Lobe** philosophy

> The judgment layer you reach for to decide which finding actually matters, how to frame it without fearmongering, and how a technical bug connects to legal, geopolitical, and humanitarian stakes.

## Reach for this when

- You are about to slap 'critical' or 'sophisticated' on something and need a proportion check before the word ships (pairs directly with the no-critical-without-hard-proof and verify-before-asserting discipline).
- You are writing the impact/business-context section of a report or disclosure and need the security-is-cost-imposition frame to translate a bug into 'how much did we raise the attacker's cost' rather than abstract severity.
- You are moving from operator to advisor/briefer: a CISO, a CERT, a healthcare org, or a government wants to know why a finding matters and what to actually fix.
- You hit an ICS/OT or medical-device exposure and need to reason about the lethality boundary case by case instead of sensationalizing it.
- You need an attribution-confidence frame: deciding whether to publicly name an operator, and how graded confidence and false-flag risk change the call.
- You are deciding what to enumerate vs exfiltrate and want the restraint ethic argued from first principles, not just policy.

## Principles

- Security is never absolute; it is the work of raising an attacker's cost high enough that the action is not worth taking. This is the one rule that 'always works' and it scales from home user to military.
- Informed skepticism is itself a security control. Most attacks in the press do not matter; a few are grave. Your job is to tell which is which and resist the reflex to call everything sophisticated.
- Threat model is relative to who you are and what you hold. The same control set is wrong for different people; model risk against the actual adversary and actual assets, not an abstract maximal threat (Insight #7 in the book, matches  threat-model-relative-to-org discipline).
- The CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity, availability) is the high-level classifier most threats sort into; use it to anchor severity language.
- Scale logic: finite attacker groups, near-infinite targets, so someone important is compromised every day by statistical certainty, not a coordinated apocalypse. That fills the news; do not read it as coordination.
- The lethality boundary is shifting and must be reasoned case by case. A typical breach cannot kill; implants, industrial automation, and life-critical systems move the line, so reason, do not sensationalize.

## Moves

- Run the cost-imposition test on any finding: state explicitly how much the bug raises or lowers the adversary's cost and effort, and frame remediation as cost-raising, not as 'fix the vuln'.
- Apply the kill-chain thought model (recon, weaponization, delivery, exploitation, installation, C2, achieve-goals) to locate where a chain can be detected and broken, then say which rung your finding sits on.
- Classify every finding into the CIA triad before assigning severity, so the impact language is anchored not adjectival.
- Use graded attribution: never assert an operator identity as certainty; state confidence level, account for false-flag possibility, and decide whether public attribution even serves a purpose before naming.
- Run the proportionality/skepticism filter on incoming reporting and on your own writeup: did the source understand the topic, is 'sophisticated' load-bearing or decorative, does the response match the graded confidence.
- For continuity/ransomware framing, recommend the 3-2-1 backup rule (three copies, two media, one offsite) as the concrete control, and reach for the Iron rules (current software, Diceware passphrases, password managers, MFA toward passwordless, compartmentalized drawers, most users need no VPN) when writing user-facing hygiene guidance.
- For ICS/medical findings, walk the lateral-movement question explicitly (the tram example: prevent jumping from an ad panel to ticket validation; do not let a peripheral system reach the safety-critical one).

## Watch out

- This is a thinking text, not a hands-on one. It gives you no working primitive, no command, no exploit. Reach for it for judgment and framing, never for execution; the offensive cards (#12, #15, #17, #25, #30, #32, #34, #35, #42, #43, #44) carry the mechanics.
- Written for a general/policy-literate reader. As an expert you will find the technical chapters (CIA triad, kill chain, MITRE intro) basic; mine the proportion, attribution, and lethality reasoning, skim the rest.
- The 'most attacks do not matter' frame can become a complacency excuse if misapplied. It is a calibration tool against fearmongering, not a license to downgrade a verified critical. Keep it on the same leash as the hard-proof-for-critical discipline.
- 'Most users do not need a VPN' and similar consumer takes are correct for the home-user threat model the chapter addresses; do not carry them into an operator/OT or adversary-emulation context where the threat model is entirely different.
- Geopolitical specifics (2022 Ukraine, US 2016, France 2017) are snapshots with a publication horizon; treat the reasoning frame as durable and the cited events as dated examples, not current state.
