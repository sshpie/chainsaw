# 16. Psybersecurity

**ISBN** 9781040111994  |  **Lobe** defensive

> The human-layer counterweight: a field manual for treating cognition, culture, and mental health as the real attack surface, and for driving security change by influence instead of mandate.

## Reach for this when

- Writing the impact section of a report and you need to make a CISO feel the risk - psybersecurity reframes harm as the mental-health, trust, and anxiety consequence the org actually fears, not just the technical CVSS number.
- Modeling a social-engineering, phishing/vishing, deepfake, or synthetic-identity chain and you need the human-target side: what cognitive bias, what trust relationship, what attention-fatigue gap the attacker exploits.
- You are the one trying to get a security finding fixed inside a hostile org and need a non-mandate playbook (Nemawashi, Cynefin guardrails, 'in tension' metrics) to move a 'Church of No' security function or get a dev team to adopt a control.
- Assessing OT/ICS where the threat model is IT-only and you need to argue the physical-safety and psychological stakes that purely technical models miss.
- Designing or evaluating a cyber range, CTF, red/blue exercise, or autonomous-agent test harness and you need the training-transfer / fidelity / psychological-readiness lens borrowed from aviation simulators.
- Reasoning about GenAI weaponization (WormGPT, voice cloning, deepfakes, synthetic identity) and the governance lag (EU AI Act, GDPR, OECD, XAI vs black-box) that lets fast harms outrun slow law.

## Principles

- The mind is the attack surface. >90% of cyberattacks (WEF 2022 Global Risks Report) exploit human vulnerabilities, so the load-bearing target is cognition and culture, not the box. 'Psybersecurity' names the case where damaging mental health IS the objective, not merely the delivery vector.
- Security culture beats security mandate. Durable change comes from influence and shared 'why', not imposed controls. Resistance is a signal to manage - too much AND too little resistance are both failures.
- Security is enablement, not gatekeeping. When teams deploy many times daily the 'Church of No' is untenable; security must scale sub-linearly via guardrails, decentralised authority, and product-minded internal-customer thinking. Security and productivity are not zero-sum.
- Habit precedes vigilance. Sustained vigilance is unsustainable even in trained security technicians, so resilient defence is built on automatic behaviour, not on asking humans to stay alert. Behaviour change follows the KAB chain: Knowledge raises Attitude raises Behaviour.
- Training is not education, and aviation is the model. Education gives principles; training gives operational readiness under pressure. Both cyber ranges and flight simulators exist to produce 'training transfer' - and most training fails to transfer (~40% immediately, ~70% after a year).
- Attention and cognitive load are finite and exploitable. Vigilance fatigue, curse-of-knowledge bias, and desire paths explain why an expert's 'obvious' control gets bypassed in the real world.

## Moves

- Classify each control with Cynefin before recommending it: complicated/well-understood controls become automated guardrails; complex/interdependent/uncertain controls get adaptive methods like collaborative threat modelling. Stop hand-mandating what should be a guardrail.
- Measure security friction with 'in tension' metrics so you can prove security and productivity are not opposed, instead of fighting the 'us versus them' divide on vibes.
- Drive a fix through a resistant org with the named toolkit: Nemawashi (pre-socialise the decision), Wardley explorers/villagers/town-planners (engage the right people at the right maturity), and Schwartz's Monkey/Razor/Sumo-Wrestler bureaucracy moves.
- Apply Theory of Constraints five focusing steps (identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, iterate) plus the Flow Framework feature/defect/debt/risk split to sequence continuous-small-steps security instead of a big-bang program. Remember hyperbolic discounting: people undervalue the future patch.
- Run threat modelling as a collaborative, gamified 'enabling constraint' using the Elevation of Privilege card game (Shostack); pair with Security Chaos Engineering (Rinehart & Shortridge) to test resilience by injecting failure deliberately.
- For human-target impact framing, map the attack onto the Psybersecurity Risk Framework (Parasuraman & Riley use/misuse/disuse/abuse, recast with the human as target) and name the mental-health outcome (anxiety, fear, depression) as the documented impact.
- Design or grade a range/agent test against the training-transfer criteria: simulation/emulation/overlay/hybrid range type, green/white/red/blue team structure, measured speed-to-objective + action correctness (Andreolini et al. 2020), wearable-captured participant pressure, and Eq 9.1 cognitive-complexity weighting. Borrow the aviation A/B method: simulator-trained vs not, against clear measurable competency criteria.

## Watch out

- Australia-centric, education-and-culture-heavy edited volume with uneven chapter depth. It is the human/organisational/governance counterweight, NOT a source of working exploit primitives. Do not reach for it expecting CVEs, payloads, or tooling.
- Light on rigorous metrics and full of round, cited-but-soft numbers (10%/75%/90% learning retention from Hallam 1955; the >90% human-vulnerability stat). Use these as rhetorical anchors for a report narrative, not as defensible quantitative evidence in a finding.
- 'Psybersecurity' is a coined, emerging term, not established field vocabulary. Define it in-line for any reader; do not assume a CISO or triager knows it.
- The aviation-simulator analogy is persuasive but a metaphor - training transfer in cockpits is heavily regulated and instrumented in ways cyber ranges are not. Treat it as a target model to argue toward, not a current-state equivalence.
- Ch 7's culture-change toolkit (Nemawashi, Wardley, Cynefin, ToC) is borrowed from DevOps/management literature and is the strongest, most reusable part - but it assumes an org with engineering maturity and deploy-many-times-daily teams. It maps poorly onto a static legacy OT shop, which is ironically where 16's own Ch 1-2 thesis bites hardest.
- Governance content (EU AI Act, GDPR, OECD) reflects a 2025 snapshot and the book itself stresses law lags harm and is jurisdiction-fragmented; do not cite its regulatory specifics as current without checking.
