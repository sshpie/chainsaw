# 29. Becoming Resilient

**ISBN** 9781637424438  |  **Lobe** defensive

> A strategy lens, not a how-to: it gives you the vocabulary and operating model to frame findings as mission-impact and time-to-reconstitute instead of CVE counts, by treating resiliency (keep running while breached, reconstitute under fire) as a discipline distinct from security, fault tolerance, and disaster recovery.

## Reach for this when

- Writing the impact section of a report and you need to move the CISO from 'we blocked X' to 'here is the mission function that stops, the minimum-essential mode that survives, and the time-to-reconstitute' - the resiliency-vs-security distinction (Chapter 4) is the load-bearing reframe.
- Modeling a compound adversary scenario: cyber deliberately timed to a hurricane, pandemic, grid sag, or geopolitical event, where the assumption is the attacker is already in, dwells ~200 days, and you must operate degraded. This is the threat model that physical duplicate-site DR does NOT cover.
- Assessing ICS/OT or critical-infrastructure resilience and you need the framing for 'what is the minimum set of mission-critical objectives we keep running when we can no longer do everything,' plus how scarce surviving bandwidth/compute/power gets reallocated against that priority list.
- Evaluating an org's recovery posture and you want to test whether their clean-restore actually prevents attacker re-entry (backdoors that reopen the door) and whether their backups replicate malware/corruption into the standby.
- Arguing for communications diversity and layered substitution as a design property: can a severed wired link be swapped for cellular/Wi-Fi/satellite/drone at one layer without breaking the apps above (Internet hourglass / narrow-waist).
- Designing or critiquing a collaborative-sharing or mutual-aid resilience scheme (neighbors/SMBs/businesses sharing surviving Wi-Fi/cellular/compute/power) and you need to treat the sharing channel itself as new attack surface.

## Principles

- Resiliency has a different threat model than security, and the threat model is the whole point: security assumes the adversary is kept out or removed fast and outages are brief; resiliency assumes the adversary defeats boundary/IdAM/IDR, dwells long (~200 days mean malware dwell), contaminates the supply chain, and you must keep operating and reconstitute WHILE still under attack.
- Minimum-essential mode plus prioritization is the operational heart: you must already know the mission-critical objective subset to keep alive and what to drop, BEFORE the event, then isolate from corrupted components, take stock of survivors, and reallocate scarce resources against agreed priorities.
- Compound, simultaneous, rehearsed events are the planning baseline, not the edge case: plan for cyber-plus-natural-disaster co-occurrence (Gray Rhino: highly probable, high impact, neglected).
- Resiliency cannot be bought as a product; it is a culture, plan, and trained capability - composable interchangeable-building-block architecture, decentralized decision-making delegated to those closest to the system, entrepreneurial mindset, and relationships that are 'far harder to imitate than purely automated software solutions.'
- Recovery must not reopen the door: cleanup/restore has to deny attacker easy re-entry, the clean validated backup is the lifeline, and you need alternatives for when it fails or when sync gaps leave you bootstrapping.
- Measurement is the unsolved problem and the reason resiliency lags - there are no good cost-effectiveness metrics against an adaptive adversary - but act now with existing primitives (encryption, anti-jamming, segmentation, backup) rather than waiting for mature metrics.

## Moves

- Run the resiliency-vs-X separation on any target: explicitly distinguish what is a security control (keep them out), a fault-tolerance control (survive component failure), a DR/BCP/COOP control (geographic duplicate site), and a true resiliency control (operate degraded and reconstitute while breached). Most orgs conflate these; the gap is the finding.
- Build the minimum-essential map BEFORE the event: critical-asset-and-process mapping plus risk-and-readiness assessment to name the prioritized mission-critical objective subset, then define what gets dropped and how scarce surviving bandwidth/compute/power is allocated against it.
- Apply the five-component framework as an assessment checklist: (1) identify mission-critical needs, (2) protect (traditional first line), (3) detect (security analytics + continuous watch), (4) respond (right tools for security AND physical failures), (5) sharing-and-recovery plan.
- Use the two resiliency proxy metrics where real metrics are missing: adversary dwell time in a deception/honeypot environment = deception effectiveness; time between compromise of one enclave/segment and a similar resource in another enclave = segmentation effectiveness. Report these as MOEs/MOPs/KPPs (how fast, how completely, with how much confidence functions reconstitute).
- Run the resiliency assessment triad: retrospective operational analysis + Red Team emulation in a limited environment + analytic measurement (broader attack coverage, less realism). No single method is sufficient.
- Pressure-test the backup logic: confirm synchronization avoids replicating malware/corruption into the standby, confirm a bootstrap plan exists for unsynced gaps, and confirm the restore path denies attacker re-entry. Choose in-house remote site vs cloud vs DRaaS (pay-per-use standby) by cost-vs-criticality.
- Probe the collaborative-sharing channel as attack surface: identity verification of sharing parties (physical tokens exchanged in advance, one-time startup access codes for mutual auth), prioritization of scarce resources by criticality, and coordination protected with encryption, steganography, and out-of-band low-probability-of-intercept comms.
- Audit communications diversity and layered substitution: count the independent paths (wired + cellular + Wi-Fi + satellite + drone, increasingly self-powered with solar/battery) and verify the hourglass narrow-waist actually lets you swap a severed link at one layer without breaking apps above; can the degraded residual default to a safer architecture (SCION isolation domains) like an OS safe mode.
- Recommend the from-scratch reconstitution kit as a concrete deliverable: secure (mobile) space + emergency equipment, startup software/protocols, computers with trusted checkpoints (possibly blockchain-backed), emergency power, stealth startup comms (drones/LEO/antennas), startup crypto codes, one-time access codes - and the practice discipline of annual isolation/response exercises building toward periodic rebuild-from-scratch.

## Watch out

- This is a 2022-2023 strategy/leadership book, NOT a technical how-to. The 5G/6G/Wi-Fi 7/satellite/PQC/QKD survey is broad-but-shallow and time-stamped - treat it as orientation, not authority. Verify any specific number (PQC winners, RSA factoring records, gang names) against current primary sources before citing.
- The collaborative-sharing-among-neighbors mechanism is aspirational and largely unbuilt; it is a concept to reason with, not a deployable architecture. Do not present it to a client as an off-the-shelf control.
- The author concedes resiliency measurement is the unsolved problem - so its metrics (MOEs/MOPs/KPPs, dwell-time and segmentation proxies) are qualitative/semi-quantitative directional indicators, not hard numbers. Do not let the proxy metrics imply false precision in a resiliency score.
- The agentic-AI fit is thin and thematic only: AI appears as asset-mapping/anomaly-detection mention with no agent architecture. Do not reach here for AI-infra technical substance - route to #24, #38, #39.
- The warship/military analogy is a rhetorical organizing device. It motivates the mindset well but does not by itself yield engineering specifications; pair it with #33 Chaos Engineering and the ICS books before any implementation claim.
- Per-sector trend chapters (Ch 2) and the comms-tech survey (Ch 1) are skip-skim; the load-bearing chapters are 4 (definitions/threat-model) and 5 (blueprint) plus the culture material in 6.
