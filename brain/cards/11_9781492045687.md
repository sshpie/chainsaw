# 11. The Art of Leadership: Small Things, Done Well

**ISBN** 9781492045687  |  **Lobe** human

> Lopp's field manual of 30 small repeatable leadership practices that compound into judgment; reach for it as the operating-system layer over how you run people, agents, and a long research program.

## Reach for this when

- You are designing an orchestrator-plus-delegation flow and need a principled answer to which tier gets which scoped task: the delegation ladder (oversight x magnitude/complexity x team size) is the same calculus as Opus-to-Sonnet-to-Haiku routing.
- A subagent or executor produced a 'B' result and you feel the urge to redo it yourself: 'a delivered B the team owns beats the A you micromanaged' is the rule that keeps you from re-running good-enough work fed rich upstream context.
- You are triaging a flood of findings, alerts, or scanner candidates and need a sorting lens: the Signal Network criticality-x-freshness grid (four quadrants, watch the Important-Slow quadrant) is a direct triage tool.
- You are running a months-long research program and discipline is slipping: 'you can't hack leadership / pick one small thing and practice it three months' reframes codify-every-survey and verify-before-asserting as compounding habits, not one-off heroics.
- You need structure for a recurring 1:1, staff meeting, postmortem, recruiting push, or distributed-meeting, and want a battle-tested template instead of inventing one.
- You are writing a self-description or onboarding doc for collaborators (human or agent): the How-to-Rands user-guide format gives the skeleton.
- Information is missing on a team and worst-case stories are filling the vacuum: the rumor / cascading-comms machinery applies.

## Principles

- You cannot hack leadership; it is thousands of small repetitions compounding into judgment. The 1:1 did not teach that 1:1s matter; the thousandth one did. Same logic as a research methodology: the surveys produce data, the repetition produces the method.
- Trust is the single binding agent under every practice. Delegation, diverse teams, and visibly letting others change your mind are all trust-construction, not soft extras.
- Accountability is not responsibility. Responsibility makes you say 'I' and erodes trust; accountability is the obligation to give account for decisions, which your team has already equipped you to do by telling you things voluntarily.
- In the absence of information, humans fill the gap with the worst possible version of the truth. One rule that explains rumors, company-wide note sharing, and cascading comms plans.
- Fires burn faster uphill: the further up the org, the more fuel, so the executive's primary job is fire prevention, not firefighting. Maps to building methodology that prevents systematic verification failures rather than chasing each one.
- Be unfailingly kind is the overarching principle (DJ the raid leader: clear repeated explanation, expert answers, never losing composure, every single time).

## Moves

- Run the delegation ladder as an agent-routing calculus: scale the tier on oversight needed, project magnitude/complexity, and team composition; a delivered B owned by the executor beats the micromanaged A.
- Triage with the Signal Network grid: plot each finding/alert on criticality (vertical) x freshness (horizontal); the four quadrants are Stale Slowness, Voluminous Spam, Critically Fresh, and the dangerous Important-Slow that hides real risk; route attention by quadrant.
- Apply the 8-question Situation workflow before any high-stakes call (am I the right person; complete context; track record of sources; inconsistencies; can I state multiple perspectives neutrally; my biases; my emotional state; am I the right person again) - a bias-and-emotion checklist that doubles as bidirectional skepticism.
- Use Act-Last / Read-the-Room / Taste-the-Soup: speak last like the poker button to gather context, open by sampling with small critical questions instead of dictating; the sampling-not-micromanaging move mirrors fingerprint-not-exfiltrate restraint.
- Block A Precious Hour every day (phone away, door closed, no to-do list) to actually build, and distinguish the real Zone from the Faux-Zone (the chemical rush of busy that produces zero creative value).
- Run a Blue Tape List on any new gig/codebase/target: log everything that feels off, wait about a month, then resolve every item or explain why not. Pairs with the postmortem reality check (only 14% of identified fixes actually get done - close the loop).
- Write a How-to-Rands user guide (Average Week, North Star Principles, Feedback Protocol, Meeting Protocol, Nuance/Errata) to accelerate any new working relationship, including a subagent task-spec template.
- Run the VST+J self-test: write your own principles in 30 minutes, revisit daily for a week, allocate 100 points each across Vision/Strategy/Tactics for Current vs Ideal, have a trusted person rank you, then use Judgment to pick the small thing to practice for three months.

## Watch out

- It is engineering-management memoir, not security or AI-infra content; value is methodological and human-facing, never technical. Do not cite it for a technical claim.
- The author states names and stories are fictionalized, so treat every number as illustrative anecdote, not citable data: the 14%-of-postmortem-fixes, 75% Asch, ~42 Must List names, Rule of 3 and 10 are vibes-with-numbers, not evidence.
- The advice is calibrated to large, well-funded eng orgs (Netscape, Apple, Slack); the 50%-of-time-on-recruiting and dedicated Meeting Historian/Runner roles do not transfer to a solo researcher or a two-person lab without heavy translation.
- Single-leader, charismatic-individual framing throughout ('how to Rands'); resist importing the cult-of-the-manager voice into 's firm/lab we/our voice.
- The team-management analogies to agent orchestration are a useful lens, not a literal mapping; agents have no morale, no trust to build, and no 30%-unhappy baseline, so do not over-anthropomorphize the delegation ladder.
