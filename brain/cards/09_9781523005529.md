# 09. Humble Leadership

**ISBN** 9781523005529  |  **Lobe** human

> A relationship-levels vocabulary (L-1/L1/L2/L2.5/L3) and situational-humility practice for getting the information that lives below the role/status layer; reach for it to diagnose why a handoff, a team, or an agent pipeline is starving on the data it needs.

## Reach for this when

- Designing an orchestrator-to-subagent task spec and a handoff keeps degrading: the Scheins frame this as a Level 1 (role-and-rule transaction) versus Level 2 (open, trust-bearing information flow) choice, and they argue complex/undefined-role work fails at Level 1, which is exactly why thin subagent specs collapse.
- Verification stage of an assessment is stalling because the finding lives below the org chart: the un-asked tech, custodian, or shift operator sees the exposure the role layer denies; the book's 'information is widely distributed and people withhold bad news from messenger-shooting bosses' theme is the social model for that  insight.
- Engaging a client and translating risk: you need to map who actually holds the load-bearing knowledge versus who has the title, and decide which relationships to deepen to get truth instead of metrics.
- A team or agent ensemble has to reach a decision and you want to anticipate failure modes: pull the eight decision methods and their named traps before you design any voting/consensus/LLM-judge step.
- You catch yourself leading by 'managing the dials' (KPIs, dashboards, scan counts) and sense you are starving the social process that surfaces the real signal.
- Reasoning about how AI changes an independent researcher's edge: the 'content/expertise gets leveled, context/process/relationships gain value' argument is a direct lens on why method and chaining are the durable moat.

## Principles

- Leadership is narrowly 'the creation and implementation of something new and better' and is deliberately separated from management/stewardship, which optimize the status quo. Naming whether a task is create-new or optimize-present tells you whether you even need Level 2.
- Situational humility is a developed 3-point skill, not a trait: accept uncertainty while staying curious, be mindfully open to what others observe, and recognize bias-driven distortion / emotional triggers.
- Match the relationship level to the work: simple clear-role tasks tolerate Level 1; the more complex, collaborative, and undefined the roles, the more Level 2 (openness and trust around the task, not friendship) becomes load-bearing.
- Transparency is not openness. Transparency is passive metric display and can be weaponized (the BCS case); openness is an active choice to reveal, listen, and respond around what matters. A wall of dashboards is not trust.
- Organizations are socio-technical systems; leaders get seduced by the technical dials and starve the social process, missing information held by people with no formal authority. Social culture is far stickier than technical culture.
- Growth and resource scarcity exert a gravitational pull toward bureaucratization and regression from Level 2 down through Level 1 to Level Minus 1 (DEC's silo collapse; the Chapter 8 allegory). Trust decays by default; it is maintained against pressure, not achieved once.

## Moves

- Run the Sentiments gut-check (antipathy / apathy / empathy / compassion, Exhibit 2.2) on any working relationship or agent-handoff to label it L-1 / L1 / L2 / L3; apathy means you are at Level 1 and complex work will leak information there.
- Apply Marquet's leader-leader language reform: replace 'Request permission to / I would like to / What should I do about' with 'I intend to / I plan to / I will,' and state the why first for complex decisions. Use it both for delegating to humans and as a template for assertive subagent task specs.
- Before designing any group/agent decision step, walk the eight decision methods (Exhibit 10.1: the plop, self-authorization, minority rule, discuss-and-vote, voting, polling, consensus testing, unanimous agreement) and pre-mortem the traps: silence is not agreement, voting creates undermining minorities, status suppresses juniors.
- Build the 9-step relationship map: self in center, circles for work/family/lost/future relationships placed by status, lines weighted by intensity then labeled by level, to decide which 1-2 relationships to deepen to get the truth a project needs. Reuse it on a client engagement to find who holds the real knowledge.
- Reallocate 20-25 percent of effort away from managing metrics toward collaborative, co-creative information sharing; the rule of thumb is the budget line for the social process.
- Use ground-up relationship building to surface withheld findings: ask a narrative-eliciting, work-relevant question you genuinely do not know the answer to (avoid favorite-animal icebreakers), then run a process analysis on what worked. The INPO ratio is the expectation-setter: about half a day to spot critical trust/openness problems, two weeks to build enough relationship to fix them.
- Run the waves-on-a-beach diagnosis on a stuck change: leadership is the cresting wave, culture the sediment, onshore wind the tailwind, offshore the headwind, change arriving through repeated wash/backwash, not linear cause-effect. Stop expecting one clever fix (the orange-cone lesson).

## Watch out

- Soft-skills case-narrative book with one chart (Exhibit 10.1 is its only real table) and no formulas; do not mine it for technique, metrics, or anything adversarial. Use it for framing and team/agent-design intuition only.
- The cited numbers are rules of thumb, not measured constants: '20-25 percent of effort,' Sutton's 'bad behavior is five times more powerful than good behavior,' and the INPO half-day/two-week ratio are heuristics. Quote them as intuitions, never as evidence.
- Several cases are illustrative or fictionalized (PNHC's CEO 'Karl Green' is fictitious; the Chapter 8 startup is an allegory). Treat them as parables, not data points, and do not generalize a single anecdote into a law.
- Founder-survivorship bias: Lee Kuan Yew, Ken Olsen, and Marquet are success-by-charisma stories; the book's own DEC and Chapter-8 regression cases admit Level 2 reverts under growth, so do not read the model as a guaranteed-win playbook.
- Personizing is offered as authentic and consent-based, but the same rapport-and-elicitation mechanics are the social-engineering attack surface (chain to #19). The technique is neutral; the ethics live in intent and authorization, which the book assumes rather than guards.
- Western/anglophone management lineage (McGregor, Lewin, NTL, Edmondson); the level framework can read as culturally loaded when mapped onto teams with different norms around hierarchy and disclosure. The L1/L2 line is fuzzier across cultures than the exhibits imply.
