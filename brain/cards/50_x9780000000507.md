# 50. Physical Fault Injection and Side-Channel Attacks on Mobile Devices

**ISBN** x9780000000507  |  **Lobe** offensive

> Shepherd et al.'s 2009-2021 survey of physical fault-injection and side-channel attacks on mobile SoCs, mapping 50+ papers to a 10-goal taxonomy, an attack-cost matrix, and six methodological honesty challenges that double as a verification-discipline manual for any black-box hardware-secured target.

## Reach for this when

- Assessing the security claim of a device, edge appliance, or AI accelerator that leans on a TEE, secure enclave, or hardware key store (TrustZone, Apple SEP, Titan M, StrongBox Keymaster) and we need the actual physical-attack envelope those guarantees do and do not cover
- Fingerprinting embedded or IoT AI-inference hardware over Shodan/Censys where the HTTP layer tells us nothing and the real trust boundary is the SoC, secure-boot chain, and on-chip crypto co-processor
- Calibrating a finding's severity against attacker cost: the book's precision/cost/skill/damage matrix tells us whether a fault primitive is a $300 testbed or a $100,000 FIB workstation, which is exactly our attacker-cost-relative-to-asset reasoning
- Reading a vendor or academic FIA/SCA result critically before we cite it: the survey's six-challenge audit (unprotected reference impls, single-target bias, redacted platforms, idealized success rates) is a refutation checklist for inflated security claims
- Reasoning about cross-core software-triggered fault attacks (VoltJockey, CLKSCREW) where a privileged software adversary fault-injects from the non-secure world, the on-chip analog of our privilege-escalation and trust-boundary work
- Scoping an authorized hardware assessment of mobile/embedded targets and we need the restraint-and-damage frame: decapsulation, over-glitching, and PoP desoldering can permanently destroy the only target we have

## Principles

- Modern density defeats the classic attack: high-frequency multi-core SoCs, micrometer wiring, BGA/PoP packaging, and mixed-signal interference make the wire-level and pin-level attacks that worked on smart cards and MCUs extremely hard, so the field shifted to non-invasive EM and software-triggered DVFS fault generation
- Every physical attack decomposes on two axes: transient vs permanent and invasive vs non-invasive, and the cost ladder runs voltage/clock glitch (cheap, low precision) to EMFI (spatial precision) to laser to focused-ion-beam/heavy-ion (six-figure cost, IC microsurgery, destroys the target)
- The 10 attack goals are the impact taxonomy: TEE code execution, secure/non-secure key recovery (AES/RSA/ECC), loading unauthorized TAs, bypassing secure-boot signature checks, bypassing run-time verification, loading data into RAM, key resetting, and instruction-level reverse engineering
- Reported success rates are mostly idealized: research overwhelmingly targets unprotected reference crypto on a single device, so a single-target 96% accuracy can collapse to 2% on a sibling board with the same IC, and absence of a published attack is security-by-obscurity, not security
- Data cost is the practical gate: exploitable faults occur at under 0.1% rates and key recovery can demand 1 to 500 million traces or chosen-plaintext queries, so an attack is only feasible where those conditions can be cheaply repeated and automated

## Chains with

- **32** - the survey breaks AES/RSA/ECC by physically faulting or leaking the implementation (DFA, DPA, CRT factoring, square-and-multiply leakage); 32 is the algorithmic depth on the same ciphers, this is how you reach them through silicon
- **37** - this is the deep physical-attack craft for exactly the embedded/IoT/smart hardware 37 catalogs as attack surface, the layer below the firmware where the secure-boot and key-store trust claims actually live or die
- **44** - Forshaw's content/encoding/transport black-box loop and crash-triage discipline is the protocol-layer sibling of this book's physical black-box characterization of an undocumented SoC under fault
- **34** - VoltJockey, CLKSCREW, and the setresuid/su fault attacks are privilege escalation expressed in hardware, faulting a kernel access-control check or string compare instead of abusing a software misconfiguration
- **42** - both are vuln-research programs that drive from primitive to weaponized impact; 50 is the physical-fault entry into the same crash-to-exploit arc 42 generalizes across software bug discovery
- **26** - ICS/OT field devices are the embedded-SoC, hard-to-replace, easy-to-brick targets where the survey's over-glitching and decapsulation damage warnings map directly onto Nick's industrial restraint ethic
- **31** - same ICS context: physical fault injection against secure-boot and crypto co-processors is the bench-level threat model for the hardened controllers 31 defends, and you only get one shot at the target
- **24** - the TEE/secure-enclave/co-processor protections this book attacks are precisely the hardware roots of trust 24 leans on to secure AI systems; 50 tells you what those roots actually resist
- **03** - the modern attacks here are deep-learning and statistical-correlation driven (CNN/template/DPA over millions of traces) and the cited countermeasure is injecting randomness into execution timing and clock, randomized algorithms on both sides of the side channel
- **28** - the survey's restraint and detectability concerns mirror deception thinking; both turn on what an adversary can observe and what observation reveals, here it is unintended EM/power emanation rather than a deliberate decoy
- **02** - the survey's honesty thesis (security-by-obscurity is not security, absence of a published attack is not safety, reference-impl results overstate) is 2's structural-and-adversarial argument made concrete in silicon
- **49** - Reverse Engineering for Beginners is the companion craft: many of these attacks require physical and software reverse engineering of the boot chain and SoC die before a single fault can be aimed, the disassembly skill that gates the physical work
