# The Next Leadership Team: How to Select, Build, and Optimize Your Top Team

ISBN: 9781000876239  |  Book #10

## One-liner
A practitioner playbook arguing there is no single best leadership-team model: a CEO must pick one of three approaches (Team of Stars, Synergistic Team, Stretch Team), match it to company type and situation, and then select, build, and optimize the team consistently across all variables.

## Thesis
Keil and Zangrillo's central claim, grounded in a five-year research project of interviews with CEOs, C-suite members, and headhunters, is that there is no one-size-fits-all top team; rather several distinct leadership-team approaches can all succeed, and consistency of implementation plus fit with the company situation matter more than which approach is chosen. Each of the three high-performance approaches (Team of Stars, Synergistic Team, Stretch Team) has a degenerate low-performance twin (shark tank, petting zoo, mediocracy), and the correct choice is contingent on company type (diversified, integrated multi-business, single business) and the leader's situational mandate (start-up, continuation and development, transformation and restart). The book then maps this 3-by-3-by-3 framework onto the full lifecycle of selecting members, building the team, and optimizing it over time.

## Structure
### Introduction
Frames the team (not the lone CEO) as the unit of corporate performance. Opens with Thierry Delaporte's Wipro turnaround (cut exec team from 20+ to 10, collapsed 27 P&L units to 4) and contrasts it with Nadella's Microsoft transformation from 'warring gangs pointing guns at each other' into a collaborative team. States the two core insights: multiple approaches can work, and fit-to-situation is decisive. Cites Alcatel-Lucent and GE 'performance theater' as failure cases.

### Part I, Chapter 1: Approaches to leadership teams
Introduces the competition-vs-collaboration tension and the three approaches via Table 1.1. Team of Stars (competition, individual units, fast decisions, risk = shark tank), Synergistic Team (collaboration, consensus, company-level optimization, risk = petting zoo), Stretch Team (balances both, mixes stars and team players, risk = mediocracy and highest failure rate). Three failure causes: ignoring each config's key risks, inconsistent implementation, and misfit with situation.

### Part I, Chapter 2: Mapping approach to situational constraints
Splits constraints into company type and company situation. Three company types (Table 2.1): diversified (Tata, Sumitomo; Team of Stars/Stretch), integrated multi-business (Unilever, P&G, HSBC; Stretch/Synergistic/Stars), single business (Uber, Tesla, Ricola; Synergistic/Stretch). Three situations and mandates (Table 2.2): start-up (leader as entrepreneur), continuation and development (leader as coach), transformation and restart (leader as decision-maker). Uses Al-Saleh at T-Systems, Meier at Ricola, Fredrickson at AstraZeneca oncology.

### Part II, Chapters 3-4: Selecting team members
Chapter 3 covers team role (information/consultation/coordination/decision), size, which organizational roles to include, diversity as strategy not compliance, internal vs external hiring trade-offs, and the leader-mandate-driven selection process. Four selection criteria categories: experience/track record, skills/competence, personal characteristics, team/organizational fit. Chapter 4 maps selection onto the three approaches (Table 4.1) and three situations (Table 4.2): Stars get larger teams and individual track record; Synergistic get smaller teams, low ego, fit; Stretch get diversity and mixed profiles.

### Part III, Chapters 5-6: Building the team
Chapter 5 lays out seven building levers: team purpose (distinct from company purpose/vision/strategy), team environment (trust in four levels, psychological safety, behavioral norms, commitment/accountability), structures/processes (RASCI, agenda, tasks), managing discussion (productive vs interpersonal conflict), decision-making (five modes from command to consensus), the leader's role (aggregator/facilitator/coach), and development tools. Chapter 6 adapts these to approach (Table 6.1) and situation (Table 6.2), e.g., Stars = manage divas/politics, Synergistic = build trust/psych safety, Stretch = strong norms and balanced discussion.

### Part IV, Chapters 7-8: Optimizing performance over time
Chapter 7 covers keeping the team aligned, performance management (intrinsic over financial incentives, behaviors plus outcomes), keeping the team relevant (six anti-complacency practices: develop, reorganize, rotate, tenure limits, diversity, set the tone), strategic change (capability-architecture vs business-model change), changing members (respectful dismissal, 'elephant graveyards,' eight toxic-member signs), and succession/talent management. Chapter 8 maps optimization to the three approaches (Table 8.1) and three situations (Table 8.2).

### Chapter 9: Designing the future of leadership teams
Six forward-looking themes: purpose-driven teams (Zurich Insurance moving from policies to principles), organizational resilience (four principles: multiple time horizons, crisis-management drills, group response/no profiteering per Shell oil-trader example, engage all layers), driving organizations through personal style (virtuous vs vicious feedback loops), the changing profile of leaders (non-linear CVs, learn-it-all over know-it-all, Narasimhan at Novartis), and creating/leveraging diversity through genuine inclusion (reverse mentoring, the 'checklist diversity' critique).

### References and Index
Extensive bibliography blending practitioner sources (Hastings/Meyer No Rules Rules, Joly Heart of Business, Chambers Connecting the Dots, Nadella Hit Refresh, Lencioni Five Dysfunctions) with academic upper-echelons and TMT literature (Hambrick, Edmondson, Eisenhardt, Finkelstein). Detailed index keyed to all named executives and concepts.

## Core ideas
- There is no single best leadership-team model; three distinct approaches (Team of Stars, Synergistic Team, Stretch Team) can each lead to high performance, and consistency of implementation beats choice of style.
- Each high-performance approach has a degenerate twin it slides into when risks are ignored: Team of Stars becomes a shark tank, Synergistic Team becomes a petting zoo, Stretch Team becomes mediocracy.
- Competition and collaboration are a genuine trade-off, not a free lunch; the Stretch Team that tries to combine both is the hardest to run and has the highest failure rate because each element is less pronounced than in the pure approaches.
- The right approach is contingent on company type: diversified companies (minimal synergies) suit a Team of Stars; single business companies (high integration) suit a Synergistic Team; integrated multi-business companies suit a Stretch Team.
- Independent of company type, the leader's situational mandate (start-up, continuation and development, transformation and restart) reshapes how the approach is implemented, casting the leader as entrepreneur, coach, or decision-maker respectively.
- Team member selection should start from a leader mandate (a strategic diagnosis of the role's future challenges), following 'structure follows strategy,' rather than from a generic or inherited role profile.
- Diversity should be treated as a strategic advantage, not a compliance checklist; 'checklist diversity' fails because organizations hire disruptors then expect them to behave like everyone else, so inclusion (making voices heard) matters as much as hiring.
- A leadership team needs a distinct team purpose, separate from company purpose, vision, and strategy, that explains why the team itself must exist; without it members treat meetings as a waste of time.
- Trust builds in four escalating levels (knowledge, commitment, intentions, vulnerability), but over-trust causes inertia, so leaders must balance trust against challenging the status quo.
- Performance management should weight behaviors alongside outcomes; financial incentives in creative senior work are often counterproductive and should mainly signal what good looks like rather than drive behavior.
- Success breeds complacency, so relevant teams need deliberate anti-ossification practices: rotation, tenure limits, reorganization, diversity, and a renewal-focused tone (Grove's 'only the paranoid survive').
- The team that starts a business is rarely the team that scales it; the very definition of 'a great team' changes with the company's situation, requiring planned member changes over time.
- Strategic change must be classified as either retaining the capability architecture/business model (develop or add a role) or modifying it (a full re-evaluation of the team), because adding a chief digital or sustainability officer is insufficient for the latter.
- The CEO's personal style creates a trickle-down feedback loop (virtuous or vicious) that propagates the team's competition-or-collaboration tone throughout the whole organization.

## Techniques and methods
- Three leadership-team approaches framework: Team of Stars / Synergistic Team / Stretch Team, plotted on a competition-vs-collaboration axis (Figure 1.1, Table 1.1) with paired low-performance variants (shark tank / petting zoo / mediocracy).
- Company-type taxonomy (Table 2.1): diversified, integrated multi-business, single business, matched to recommended approaches via degree of diversification and interdependence.
- Situation-and-mandate taxonomy (Table 2.2): start-up (leader as entrepreneur), continuation and development (leader as coach), transformation and restart (leader as decision-maker).
- Leader mandate method: derive each role's profile from a strategic diagnosis of future challenges, then use the mandate to evaluate incumbents, select new members, and measure performance ('structure follows strategy,' Chandler).
- Four selection criteria categories: experience/track record, specific skills/capabilities, personal characteristics, fit with team and organization; plus Chambers' six hiring criteria and Amey's three 'buckets' (toolkit, leadership capabilities, leader's shadow).
- Four selection questions: Are changes needed? Who to replace? How quickly? Who to hire instead (internal vs external pros/cons lists).
- Four dimensions of team environment: trust (four levels: knowledge, commitment, intentions, vulnerability), psychological safety (Edmondson), agreed behavioral norms, commitment and accountability.
- Five decision-making modes: command-based, advisory, collaborative vote, collaborative compromise, collaborative consensus.
- RASCI / RACI responsibility-assignment model (Responsible, Accountable, Supporting, Consulted, Informed).
- Value-creating vs value-destroying meeting behaviors with a 'red card' mechanism (Hintikka at WithSecure).
- Six practices to keep a team relevant: develop individuals/team, reorganize structure, rotate members, institute tenure limits (Hilti's 3-year contracts up to 15 years max), maintain diversity, set the tone.
- Capability-architecture vs business-model change classification (Henderson and Clark) and a six-question test for identifying industry-redefining change.
- Eight signs of a toxic team member (e.g., 'talks in the hall but not in the room,' adopts victim mentality, criticizes others in public).
- Four resilience-building principles: multiple time horizons (temporal ambidexterity), trained crisis-management processes, group response with no profiteering, engage all organizational layers.
- Proactive talent pipelining: meeting one external candidate per month to build a vetted external bench (Fredrickson at AstraZeneca).

## Memorable
- Jan Jenisch's football metaphor for a Team of Stars: 'a team of eleven Ronaldos. With such a team you give them each a task, let them run and then follow up.'
- Hintikka on hiring: 'If you have people with the wrong values but high competence, they will very competently lead the organization in the wrong direction... it's better to take on the people with the right values and lower competence, because they can learn.'
- Wipro: Delaporte cut the executive team from 20+ to 10, reduced 27 P&L units to 4 units with two business lines and four functions, over 18 months, reinforcing Wipro's 'Five Habits.'
- Nadella described Microsoft's pre-2014 culture as 'a group of warring gangs pointing guns at each other'; Microsoft became the third company after Apple and Amazon to reach a US$1 trillion valuation.
- Hilti's tenure rule: 'three-year contracts up to a maximum of 15 years' on the executive board.
- Freudenberg incentive split: top executives have 75 percent of bonus based on their unit, 25 percent on group performance; in T-Systems Al-Saleh phased to 75 percent common / 25 percent individual goals in the transformation's second half.
- Zümpel's high-jump analogy for goal setting: a bar at 1.20m is no challenge, 2.50m won't even be attempted, but 1.80-1.90m makes you stretch.
- Steve Jobs' principle that A-players collaborate with A-players but resist B or C-players; the book reframes a modern A-player as one who 'learns fast, is a high performer, but also able to adjust to working in a team.'
- Andy Grove's 'Only the paranoids survive' as the model for a renewal-focused team tone.
- Jenisch on the leader's role: 'I see myself as the lowest person in the company. I turn the hierarchy upside down... My job is not to direct people. My job is to multiply myself.'
- Jacqueline Hunt's 'checklist diversity' critique: organizations 'tick off that we have 30 percent female... then expect them to behave like everybody else,' and a leader who would not have interviewed a female candidate had she been male.
- Poux-Guillaume on dismissal: 'When we have to let someone go my principle is to do it quickly and do it generously... There is actually a quite large number of people that we exited that I recommended for their next job.'
- The 'elephant graveyard' phenomenon (notably in Germany): keeping deserving older leaders in low-influence roles until retirement, which Mayr calls 'highly problematic' because 'the most demotivating thing for people is to have a boss that does not perform.'
- Greco's transformation metric at Zurich: he used the quality and quantity of discussion in biweekly executive committee meetings as his 'temperature check' on progress.
- Nadella's 'learn-it-all rather than a know-it-all culture' as the defining future leadership trait.

## Curriculum fit
This book is the leadership/organizational-design pillar of the reading list, and it pairs unusually well with agentic-AI research because its core structure is a direct analogue to multi-agent orchestration. The three team approaches (competition-heavy Team of Stars, consensus-heavy Synergistic Team, balanced Stretch Team) map cleanly onto the orchestrator-plus-subagent design choices in the  methodology: when to let independent agents run their own lanes with minimal coordination versus when to force collaboration and consensus, and the recognition that 'enforcing collaboration in every situation can be counterproductive' is the same insight that justifies parallel fan-out over serialized agents. The capability-architecture vs business-model change distinction and the situational-mandate taxonomy give a vocabulary for deciding when an AI agent system needs incremental role additions versus full re-architecture. For leadership and creativity, the book is a strong source on diversity as a generator of better decisions, on psychological safety and productive-vs-destructive conflict (Edmondson, Eisenhardt) for running adversarial review and red-team processes, and on the consistency principle that ceiling is set at the design/selection stage, which echoes the 'intelligence at input' descending-orchestration finding. Its weakness for a security-research curriculum is that it is entirely non-technical and offers nothing on adversarial methods, threat modeling, or tooling; treat it as a management/process layer that informs how to compose and run human and agent teams, not as security content. Highest-leverage takeaways to carry forward: the leader-mandate-before-profile selection discipline, the eight toxic-member signs as a behavioral-anomaly checklist, and the four resilience principles as a template for hardening any standing process against shocks.
