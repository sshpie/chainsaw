## One-liner

A practitioner's field manual that walks the reader from understanding how insecure-by-design industrial control systems work, through attacking a representative ICS in a lab, to building a layered defense-in-depth program around the Purdue model and an IDMZ.

## Thesis

ICS were built decades ago for reliability and safety, not security, on protocols (Modbus, PROFINET, S7comm, EtherNet/IP) that carry no authentication or encryption; the long-held "security by obscurity / air gap" assumption is now false because of IT/OT convergence. Because the underlying field devices usually cannot be patched, hardened, or even safely scanned, you cannot secure them directly. Instead you must apply a defense-in-depth model: segment the plant per the Purdue/CPwE reference architecture, broker all enterprise-to-industrial traffic through an Industrial DMZ, stack independent compensating controls (network, computer, application, device, plus policy/awareness), and wrap it all in a NIST 800-82 / ISA-62443 risk-management program. The book is structured so the reader first sees the attacker's view of how trivially an ICS falls, then learns each defensive layer, because understanding the offense is what makes the defense convincing.

## Structure

- Part 1 - Foundations (Ch.1-3): defines ICS, its view/monitor/control functions and components (PLC, HMI, SCADA, DCS, SIS); the insecure-by-inheritance protocol family; and the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture (zones, 6 levels, IDMZ at 3.5) plus the CPwE Cisco/Rockwell architecture, anchored by the fictional Slumbertown Paper Mill
- Part 2 - The attacker's perspective (Ch.4-6): a full hands-on offensive walkthrough against a lab ICS - reconnaissance with Shodan/Nmap/p0f, exploitation with Metasploit/Meterpreter/Scapy/smod, pivoting and persistence with Mimikatz/incognito/psexec, plus risk-assessment theory and the (ICS) Cyber Kill Chain
- Part 3 - Defense-in-depth introduced (Ch.7 transition / risk + kill chain): risk = likelihood x impact, threat modeling, gap-vs-vulnerability-vs-risk assessment, and the six-layer defense-in-depth onion with the AIC (not CIA) triad for ICS
- Part 4 - Building each defensive layer (Ch.7-11): Physical, Network (segmentation/conduits, Cisco ASA firewalls, DLR ring, AlienVault OSSIM SIEM, NXLog, OSSEC), Computer (endpoint hardening, EMET, AppLocker, WSUS, Symantec CSP whitelisting, host firewall, anti-malware), Application (the vulnerability taxonomy, OpenVAS scan of Metasploitable, secure SDLC), and Device (hardening, signed firmware, life-cycle phases)
- Part 5 - The security program (Ch.12): tying it together with NIST SP 800-82 / ANSI-ISA-62443, the Risk Management Framework, policies/standards/guidelines/procedures, and a worked Slumbertown initial risk assessment with prioritized mitigation - closing on the four-step 'know it, fix it, rinse and repeat' creed
- A recurring fictional case study (Slumbertown Paper Mill, characters George and Mark) threads every chapter, turning abstract controls into a concrete plant with a real attack and a real remediation plan

## Core ideas

1. Insecure by inheritance: ICS protocols were designed in an era of trusted, isolated networks, so Modbus (1979, port 502), PROFINET, S7comm (port 102), and EtherNet/IP+CIP (port 44818) ship with no authentication, no encryption, and no integrity checks - the vulnerability is baked into the design, not a bug to be patched
2. The Purdue model is the organizing spine of the whole book: 6 levels (0 Process, 1 Basic Control, 2 Area Supervisory, 3 Site Operations, 4 Site Business, 5 Enterprise) split into Enterprise and Industrial/Manufacturing zones, with the Industrial DMZ (IDMZ, level 3.5) as the single brokered boundary - no traffic should ever pass directly between enterprise and plant
3. Defense-in-depth as a security onion: because no single control is sufficient and field devices cannot be secured directly, you stack independent layers (Physical, Network, Computer, Application, Device + Policies/Procedures/Awareness) so a breach of one layer is caught by the next; the book devotes one chapter to each layer
4. The AIC triad inverts the IT CIA priority: for ICS, Availability comes first, then Integrity, then Confidentiality - keeping the process running and safe outweighs secrecy, which reshapes every control decision (you accept a less-locked-down posture rather than risk downtime or unsafe state)
5. The security bubble analogy: since the most sensitive, oldest devices cannot be hardened, you instead get them out of harm's way - isolate them onto their own network (priority 1), restrict and lock down all access (priority 2), and only allow interaction through a secured, monitored conduit; this is also exactly how the Slumbertown mitigation is prioritized
6. You cannot secure what you don't know you have: accurate, continuously updated asset inventory (software versions, patch levels, firmware revisions) is repeatedly named the highest-priority and foundational activity - it precedes vulnerability testing, patching, whitelisting, and the risk assessment
7. Patching is mostly a manual, test-first discipline in the industrial zone: uptime requirements (99.999%+) and 20-30 year device lifetimes mean most level-2-and-below systems cannot reboot or be auto-updated, so where patching is impossible you compensate with application whitelisting and firewall isolation rather than leaving the gap open
8. Whitelisting beats blacklisting for stagnant ICS hosts: blacklisting (allow all, deny a list) is easily bypassed and fits frequently-changing level-3+ systems; whitelisting (deny all, allow a list, ideally by cryptographic hash + digital signature) fits the purpose-built, rarely-changing level-2-and-below hosts and even stops 0-day exploits
9. Never scan a live ICS: any network scan can crash a 25-year-old Ethernet stack on a PLC, so all reconnaissance, vulnerability scanning, and pentesting must be done on a representative test network, never production - a discipline the author repeats across the offensive and application-security chapters
10. Security must be built in early across the life cycle (secure SDLC for custom HMI/code, and procurement-through-decommissioning for devices) because retrofitting security after a system is in production is far harder and costlier - 'there are serious risks in deploying systems on the assumption that they will be secured later'
11. A security program, not point fixes: real ICS security is a tailored, repeating program grounded in NIST SP 800-82 and ANSI/ISA-62443, driven from policies down through standards, guidelines, and procedures, executed via the Risk Management Framework (categorize, select controls, assess, implement) and a continuous improvement cycle
12. IT/OT convergence and culture gap: because plant and business networks are now connected, ICS risk is just another enterprise risk - but securing it requires a cross-functional team (IT, control engineer, operator, safety expert, risk staff, vendor) with senior-management buy-in, since IT and controls cultures differ sharply

## Techniques and methods

- Network segmentation with zones and conduits per ISA-99/CPwE: assign trust levels, separate Industrial from Enterprise physically and logically, divide the industrial network into cells/areas/enclaves, and route every cross-boundary flow through IDMZ broker services
- Building the IDMZ in practice: Cisco ASA firewalls in active/standby, dedicated VLANs, a WSUS server in the IDMZ that pulls Microsoft updates and serves industrial-zone clients (TCP 8530) with explicit permit rules and optional URL filtering
- Centralized monitoring and logging: stand up an AlienVault OSSIM SIEM, ship logs with NXLog CE, run OSSEC HIDS with custom decoders and rules, and use syslog severity levels 0-7 - with the caveat that someone must actually watch the alerts ('detection is a must')
- Endpoint hardening: narrow the attack surface (disable unused Windows services, uninstall apps, remove sample files) and limit blast radius by running services under dedicated restricted accounts (the worked FileZilla srv_FileZillaFTP example replacing Local System)
- Compensating controls on un-patchable hosts: Microsoft EMET protection profiles (API/shim hooking via the application-compatibility framework, with Rockwell's precompiled XML profile), Microsoft AppLocker whitelisting, and Symantec Embedded Security/Critical System Protection with hash+publisher rules, sandboxing, trusted updaters, USB device control, and prevention-vs-detection policies
- Host-based firewalls and configuration/change management: enforce Windows Firewall via Group Policy at boot, start every system from a known-secure baseline configuration, and track every deviation through change control
- Vulnerability assessment workflow: OpenVAS (apt install openvas, openvas-setup/-feed-update/-start, web UI on :9392) scanning Metasploitable, triaging by severity, and verifying a finding by exploiting it (ncat to the port-1524 backdoor for a root shell); Nessus named as the commercial counterpart
- The application vulnerability taxonomy as a checklist (input validation, software tampering, authentication, authorization, insecure configuration, session management, parameter manipulation) each paired with its common attacks and concrete defenses; secure SDLC integrates threat modeling, code review, and pentest into the dev life cycle
- Device hardening and life cycle: disable diagnostic web portals/telnet/SSH/SNMP or firewall them (Tofino, Cisco ISA, Rockwell Stratix 5950), lock cabinets and block ports, build in redundancy (power, comms, I/O, controllers) for availability, prefer cryptographically signed firmware (Rockwell ControlLogix rev 20+), and manage procurement-install-operation-decommission with NIST 800-88 media sanitization
- The defensive reading of the Cyber Kill Chain: map Lockheed Martin's 7 stages and the SANS two-phase ICS Cyber Kill Chain onto your environment to decide where to detect and break the chain
- Risk-program method: gap analysis (controls vs NIST recommended) + vulnerability assessment + full risk assessment producing risk scores; run the RMF and a once-or-twice-yearly improvement cycle; track issues with SimpleRisk and forensics with a SIEM (Tripwire Log Center / AlienVault)
- Offensive toolchain for the lab phase, used to prove the defense matters: Shodan and Nmap (modbus-discover.nse, enip-info) for recon, p0f for passive fingerprinting, Scapy+smod and CPPPO for protocol manipulation, Metasploit/Meterpreter/msfvenom for exploitation, and Mimikatz/incognito/psexec for credential theft and lateral movement

## Memorable

- The book's running case study is the Slumbertown Paper Mill (opened 1911, three production lines, a digester running over 200 degrees F) with a test network of internet 122.10.10.0, business 10.10.10.0, ICS 172.25.30.0 and domain SlumbertownMill.local; characters George (an IT/OT pro since 1970) and Mark (who clicks a phishing link to ems.net/climb) personify the human attack surface
- 'Only two things can survive a nuclear bomb: cockroaches and OPC servers' - the author's quip on the absurd longevity and fragility of legacy ICS software
- Dr. Eric Cole's maxim, quoted as the monitoring chapter's thesis: 'Prevention is ideal, but detection is a must'
- The first PLC was the Modicon 184 (1975) by Dick Morley; Modbus dates to 1979 - underscoring that today's plants still run protocols and device lineages designed before modern networking security existed
- A 'freebie mini pentest' against a real Telenet N.V. (Belgium) device found via Shodan walks through reading a 1756-ENBT card and surfaces a cleartext password literally set to 'VerySecretPa$word'
- WannaCry is cited as infecting roughly 300,000 computers across 200 countries, with Windows XP still at 5.66% market share - the concrete argument for why un-patchable ICS endpoints are a systemic problem
- Real malware and CVE anchors throughout: Stuxnet, Crash Override (Ukraine 2015), NotPetya, EternalBlue (MS17-010 / CVE-2017-0143, NSA via Shadow Brokers), CVE-2016-9158 on the Siemens S7-400 scored 7.6, and CVE-2012-0507
- CyberX in 2015 modified the web-server code in a Rockwell MicroLogix 1100 PLC's firmware to reach the PLC internals and discover the FrostyURL vulnerability - the book's flagship software-tampering example
- Michael Howard's rule from Writing Secure Code is invoked verbatim - 'All input is evil' - to frame the input-validation section, alongside the estimate that 85% of attacks now target application vulnerabilities
- The Symantec CSP demo deliberately omits Symantec from the publisher whitelist so the author's own Event Viewer and a copy of netcat are blocked from running, proving whitelisting stops even unknown executables and 0-days dead
- The closing creed distilling the entire book into four lines: 'Know what you have / Know what is wrong with what you have / Fix or defend what you know is wrong / Rinse and repeat'

## Curriculum fit

Foundational, practitioner-grade primer for an ICS/OT security curriculum; best placed early-to-mid as the bridge between general IT security and OT specialization. Strongest as the on-ramp to the standards it operationalizes - NIST SP 800-82, ANSI/ISA-62443 (formerly ISA-99), and the Rockwell/Cisco CPwE architecture - and as a hands-on lab companion (Kali, Metasploitable, OpenVAS, AlienVault, the Slumbertown scenario) for teaching the Purdue model, the IDMZ, and defense-in-depth by doing. Pairs naturally before deeper SANS ICS material (ICS410/ICS515) and the SANS ICS Cyber Kill Chain, and before vendor-specific PLC/SCADA hardening guides. Caveats for a 2026 syllabus: it is a 2017 first edition, so several specifics are dated - EMET reached end of life (2018), the Symantec/OpenVAS product lines and links have shifted, and CVSS/CVE references are of their era; supplement with current 62443 parts, NIST 800-82r3, and modern SIEM/EDR and OT-IDS (asset-discovery, passive monitoring) tooling. The book's enduring value is conceptual discipline - asset inventory first, never scan production, AIC over CIA, build security in early, segment-and-broker, and run a repeating risk program - which maps cleanly onto the AI/OT-infrastructure assessment work in this codebase (Purdue zoning, IDMZ brokering, verification-before-claiming, and defense-in-depth as the framing for findings).
