# 47. OSINT Techniques: Resources for Uncovering Online Information (11th Ed.)

## What this book actually is

The 11th edition (2024) of Michael Bazzell and Jason Edison's `OSINT Techniques`, ~300,000 words, 47 chapters. Bazzell is ex-FBI Cyber Crimes Task Force and the technical advisor for season one of `Mr. Robot`; Edison is an active metropolitan-PD cyber-crime lead. It is the field-standard reference for turning a single identifier into a fully attributed human or organization, used as required reading in government academies and university courses. The book is a guidebook, not a narrative: read by chapter when a specific need arises.

## Structure (where to look)

**Section I, Preparation (ch 1-15, lines ~300-8800).** The clean-room doctrine. Disposable per-investigation VMs (rebuilt Debian OSINT VM, build scripts), host hardening, OSINT browsers and extensions, and the operating posture: VPN in the target region, covert-account hygiene, contamination isolation so evidence survives a motion of discovery. The recurring thesis, self-reliance, starts here: never trust a tool you do not control.

**Custom Search Tools (ch 16, line 8855).** The tooling heart. Build and self-host your own HTML query tools because third-party aggregators get taken down, vanish, or log your searches. The method is to derive the static query URL behind any site's form (the worked example is `whoxy.com/search.php?email=`, an undocumented free endpoint) and wire it into a parameterized tool. This is the human ancestor of the garlic toolkit and aimap dork tables: fetch the real endpoint, parameterize, batch.

**Section II, the pivot arsenal (ch 17-35).** Every identifier type with named free tools and static URLs. Search engines and dorks (ch 17). Social networks (Facebook, X, Instagram, TikTok). Email (ch 23) with Holehe email-to-account. Usernames (ch 24) with Sherlock, SocialScan, Maigret across 2800+ sites and explicit false-positive caution. People-search engines, phone numbers, maps. Images and documents with ExifTool metadata extraction (`exiftool * -csv`) and reverse-image (Yandex, TinEye, Google). **Domain Names (ch 32, line 18355)** is the operator-attribution core: ViewDNS Whois, reverse-IP, reverse-Whois, port scan, IP history, DNS report (all queryable by static URL), plus Whoxy and Whoisology historical records that defeat privacy masking by surfacing the registrant name, email, and phone from an era before the domain went private, then cluster every other domain sharing those fields. **IP Addresses (ch 33, line 19264)**: reverse-IP co-hosting, IP-location, Bing `ip:`, That's Them, I Know What You Download, Exonerator (Tor-exit check), Wigle (SSID/MAC geolocation), and a full Shodan walkthrough (banner indexing, `geo:`, `city:`, device filters). Domains (ch 32) and IPs (ch 33) are the chapters  reaches for most: a Shodan or Censys hit becomes a named operator here.

**Section III, Breaches, Leaks, Logs (ch 36-44, lines ~21900-25900).** Data leaks, data breaches, and the author's favorite, **Stealer Logs (ch 43, line 24020)**: the Redline/Vidar log file tree dissected field by field (HWID, autofills, cookies for session hijack, DomainDetects, InstalledSoftware, Passwords). The investigative-benefit framing is explicit: InstalledSoftware reveals a target's VPN, hardware, and games, which is gold for OPSEC profiling and social-engineering setup. Queries, scripts, and self-hosted databases close the section.

**The close (ch 45-47, lines 25910-end).** **Methodology and Workflow (ch 45)**: intake, triage (articulate the question, verify handed-over identifiers, "information is not intelligence until it has context"), knolling the toolset, premium-then-open-source sequencing, capture-with-provenance into an organized notebook. **Documentation and Reporting (ch 46)**. **Policy, Ethics, and Development (ch 47, line 26924)**: the verbatim  anchor. The misconfigured Elasticsearch reachable with no credentials is legally accessible, but "we are taking advantage of a mistake," and intent decides appropriateness; the reasonable-expectation-of-privacy test, covert-account and infiltration ethics, CSAM handling, and restraint as tradecraft.

## Why it earns a slot

It is the attribution layer the rest of the offensive lobe lacks. The fingerprint tells us what a host is; this book tells us who runs it, and bakes in the enumerate-not-exfiltrate restraint as an ethics chapter, not an afterthought.
