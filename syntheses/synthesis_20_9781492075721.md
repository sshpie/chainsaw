# Practical Fairness: Achieving Fair and Secure Data Models

ISBN: 9781492075721  |  Book #20

## One-liner
A working data scientist's hands-on field guide to building fair, private, and secure ML systems, pairing the legal and ethical framing of fairness with runnable Python (mostly IBM AIF360/AIX360/ART) across the full pre-, in-, and post-processing pipeline.

## Thesis
Fairness in machine learning is not a single metric to optimize but a multidimensional engineering and governance problem spanning antidiscrimination, privacy, security, transparency, autonomy, and legitimacy, and it can and must be addressed with concrete technical interventions at every stage of the data science pipeline. Nielsen argues that there is no free lunch: no single fairness, explainability, or robustness method suffices, verification and human/domain review remain load-bearing, and because markets and reputation fail to deliver fairness, law is ultimately the most reliable arbiter. The book's purpose is to give practitioners both the vocabulary (legal, statistical, ethical) and the working code to actually intervene, rather than to theorize.

## Structure
### Ch 1-2: Framing fairness and metrics
Defines fairness as a cluster of domains (antidiscrimination, privacy, security, transparency, legitimacy, accountability, autonomy, minority rights, distributional concerns). Distinguishes equity vs equality, disparate treatment vs disparate impact, and introduces the data science pipeline as a sequence of points where fairness concerns enter. Catalogs equity metrics (disparate impact ratio, difference in mean outcomes, equality of odds/opportunity, statistical parity, calibration, balanced error rate, consistency, differential fairness), privacy frameworks (Solove's taxonomy, Nissenbaum's contextual integrity, k-anonymity, differential privacy), and security measures. Frames fairness as an old engineering problem (Kranzberg's laws, Gilded Age, child labor).

### Ch 3-4: Fair data and pre-processing
Covers choosing appropriate data, ensuring data integrity (proxies, true measurements, proportionality and sampling, data duplication, external validity), and quality assurance for discrimination using the 80%/four-fifths/adverse impact rule, risk difference, risk ratio, and alpha-discriminatory rules. Then walks the AIF360 pipeline and pre-processing interventions: suppression, relabeling/massaging, reweighting (statistical independence), Learning Fair Representations (Zemel et al. prototypes), and Optimized Pre-Processing (Calmon et al. distortion function), with runnable code on German credit, COMPAS, and the Adult/US Census data set.

### Ch 5-6: In-processing and post-processing
In-processing modifies the training objective: the Prejudice Remover regularizer (Kamishima et al., with the prejudice/underestimation/negative-legacy framing) and Adversarial Debiasing (Zhang et al., GAN-style adversary predicting the protected attribute). In-processing beyond antidiscrimination touches distributional concerns, minority rights, federated learning, and differential privacy. Post-processing adjusts a trained (possibly black-box/proprietary) model's outputs: Equality of Opportunity (Hardt et al., solved via linear programming with scipy) and Calibration-Preserving Equalized Odds (Pleiss et al., via randomization), using the medical/MEPS data set.

### Ch 7-8: Auditing, interpretability, and explainability
Model auditing covers white-box vs black-box, audit scoping, the Google/Raji et al. internal audit framework (scoping, mapping, artifact collection, testing, reflection), FMEA, rules vs standards. Black-box auditing for indirect influence (Adler et al., epsilon-obscure, earthmover distance) via the Black-BoxAuditing module on the Ricci data set (Ricci v. DeStefano). Ch 8 separates interpretation from explanation (Cynthia Rudin's critique of the accuracy-vs-interpretability tradeoff), builds interpretable GLRMs (Wei et al., FeatureBinarizer + LogisticRuleRegression in AIX360), then explainability via LIME (Ribeiro et al.), SHAP/Shapley (Lundberg & Lee, KernelExplainer), and data-driven ProtoDash (MMD). Closes with explainability metrics, what these miss (Mittelstadt et al.: human explanations are contrastive, selective, social), the No Free Lunch theorem, and adversarial attacks that fool LIME/SHAP (Slack et al.).

### Ch 9-10: Privacy and security of ML models
Privacy: right to be forgotten/deletion (GDPR Article 17), then attacks. Membership inference (Shokri et al.) is implemented in full PyTorch on CIFAR-10 using shadow models and a per-class attack model, achieving just under 60% accuracy vs 50% chance, demonstrating privacy is not binary. Also model inversion, model extraction, data leaks, and defenses (differential privacy libraries, k-anonymity). Security: adversarial evasion attacks (Goodfellow panda-to-gibbon, Eykholt stop-sign stickers, Chen et al. HopSkipJumpAttack via IBM ART), defenses (adversarial training, provably robust models), poisoning/backdoor/clean-label attacks (Shafahi, Steinhardt certified defenses), and packages CleverHans, Foolbox, ART, secml.

### Ch 11-12: Fair product design and the law
Ch 11 moves downstream to product: reasonable expectations, moving-target expectations, fiduciary/information-fiduciary obligations, value creation, complex systems, products that work worse for dark-skinned and female users (heart-rate optics, voice recognition), and Mathur et al.'s seven dark-pattern categories (sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, scarcity, obstruction, forced action), ending in a fair-products checklist organized by accountability, autonomy, positive contribution, real problem, transparency, proof. Ch 12 argues law is the ultimate arbiter (markets fail via privacy paradox, opacity, externalities), surveys GDPR, CCPA, data-broker laws, Algorithmic Accountability Act 2019, HIPAA, FTC, tort law, due process, right to explanation, freedom of information, biometrics (Illinois BIPA, Patel v. Facebook $550M settlement), facial-recognition bans, and California's chatbot disclosure law.

## Core ideas
- Fairness is multidimensional, not a single metric: antidiscrimination, privacy, security, transparency, legitimacy, accountability, autonomy, minority rights, and distributional concerns are distinct domains that can conflict, so optimizing one (e.g. accuracy or one disparity ratio) can silently harm another.
- The intervention timeline structures the whole field: every fairness fix lands at pre-processing (fix the data), in-processing (fix the objective/training), or post-processing (fix the outputs), and each has different access requirements, especially whether you can touch the model or only its inputs/outputs.
- Post-processing exists precisely because real-world models are often black-box or proprietary; equality-of-opportunity and calibration-preserving equalized-odds adjust a frozen model's thresholds/outputs, but you cannot have both calibration and equalized odds in general (the Pleiss et al. impossibility).
- Verification and domain review are load-bearing, not optional: an interpretable GLRM can produce a rule that is technically readable yet nonsensical (the no-cancer/no-ADHD/not-pregnant rule), so interpretability is a starting point for human supervision, not an endpoint.
- Interpretation (the model itself is transparent) is fundamentally different from explanation (post hoc analysis of an opaque model); Rudin argues the field's bias toward black-box-plus-explanation is often unjustified because two equally accurate models can disagree on their explanations.
- Human explanations are contrastive, selective, and social (Mittelstadt et al.), so technical feature-attribution methods that give a static global weight miss what people actually want from an explanation; explanation will remain an iterative human process.
- Privacy is probabilistic, not binary: a membership-inference attack that does only ~60% vs 50%-chance still constitutes a real privacy breach, and over-parameterized/overfit models leak more because they memorize.
- Attackers do not need the original training data: Shokri-style shadow models trained on synthetic, statistics-based, or noisy real data reproduce the model's behavior well enough to train an attack model, collapsing the assumed barrier to membership inference.
- Evasion and poisoning attacks are two faces of the same vulnerability: both exploit gradients near decision boundaries; evasion pushes a single example across the boundary at inference, poisoning moves the boundary itself at training time, and overfit/kinky boundaries are easier to attack.
- No Free Lunch governs the entire book: no single fairness metric, explainability method, or robustness defense works universally, which is why relying on one test (commonly just LIME or SHAP) is exploitable, as Slack et al. show by cloaking a biased classifier from both.
- Products encode fairness downstream: dark patterns, products that work worse for women and dark-skinned users in consolidated markets, fiduciary power imbalances, and value-destroying digitization are fairness failures even when the underlying model is technically sound.
- Markets and reputation cannot deliver fairness (privacy paradox, product opacity, affected parties being externalities not buyers, lack of competition), so law is the most reliable arbiter, despite law's own historical failures (Muller v. Oregon, 1926 racial-covenant ruling).
- Legal exposure for ML is already concrete and unevenly distributed: GDPR (up to 4% global turnover or 20M euro) dwarfs CCPA ($2,500/$7,500 per violation), and biometric laws like Illinois BIPA produced Facebook's $550M Patel settlement at $1,000 per violation.
- The book is deliberately tool-opinionated, standardizing on IBM's open-source stack (AIF360 for bias metrics/mitigation, AIX360 for explainability, ART for adversarial robustness) so one familiar API carries the reader across the whole pipeline.

## Techniques and methods
- Fairness intervention timeline: classify any mitigation as pre-processing, in-processing, or post-processing
- 80%/four-fifths/adverse-impact rule plus risk difference, risk ratio, selection rates, and alpha-discriminatory rules for data-set discrimination QA
- AIF360 pipeline: load standard datasets (GermanDataset, CompasDataset, AdultDataset, MEPSDataset19) and compute disparate impact, statistical parity difference, equal-opportunity difference
- Reweighting for statistical independence between protected attribute and label as a pre-processing fix
- Learning Fair Representations (Zemel et al.): map inputs to intermediate prototypes that obscure the protected attribute
- Optimized Pre-Processing (Calmon et al.): transform the data under a distortion function constraining individual change
- Prejudice Remover (Kamishima et al.): add a prejudice-index regularizer to the loss; addresses prejudice, underestimation, negative legacy
- Adversarial Debiasing (Zhang et al.): train a predictor jointly against an adversary that tries to recover the protected attribute from predictions
- Equality of Opportunity post-processing (Hardt et al.): solve for group-specific thresholds via linear programming with scipy
- Calibration-Preserving Equalized Odds (Pleiss et al.): randomized output adjustment trading off calibration and equalized odds
- Google/Raji et al. internal audit framework: scoping, mapping, artifact collection, testing, reflection; plus FMEA and rules-vs-standards framing
- Black-Box Auditing for indirect influence (Adler et al.): measure accuracy drop after making a feature epsilon-obscure; earthmover distance; F-test equivalence; BBA.load_data('ricci')
- GLRM interpretable model: AIX360 FeatureBinarizer(negations=True, returnOrd=True) then LogisticRuleRegression(lambda0, lambda1, useOrd=True), reading rules off coefficient magnitude
- LIME (Ribeiro et al.): fit a local sparse linear surrogate around a point; LimeTabularExplainer.explain_instance with num_features and top_labels
- SHAP (Lundberg & Lee): Shapley-value attribution guaranteeing accuracy/missingness/consistency; KernelExplainer = Linear LIME + Shapley with a background dataset to simulate missing features
- ProtoDash (data-driven explanation): minimize maximum mean discrepancy to select weighted prototype examples; requires features scaled to [-0.5, 0.5]
- Membership inference (Shokri et al.): train multiple shadow models on synthetic/statistics-based/noisy real data, record (last-layer outputs, train/test label, class), train a per-class attack model; full PyTorch run_attack.py / classifier_methods.py / cnn_model.py structure on CIFAR-10
- HopSkipJumpAttack (Chen et al. via ART HopSkipJump): query-efficient decision-based evasion using binary search to the boundary, Monte Carlo gradient estimate at the boundary, and a sized jump; norms L0/L1/L2/L-infinity
- Adversarial training and provably robust architectures as evasion defenses; outlier removal, domain-limited values, production noise, and Steinhardt et al. certified upper bounds as poisoning defenses
- Fair-products checklist organized by accountability, autonomy, positive contribution, real problem, transparency, proof; plus the seven dark-pattern categories as a design red-flag list

## Memorable
- No Free Lunch theorem (quoted): 'any two optimization algorithms are equivalent when their performance is averaged across all possible problems' - used to justify why no single fairness/explainability/robustness method suffices.
- Membership inference result: attack identifies training vs test membership at just under 60% accuracy where random guessing is ~50%, on CIFAR-10 with 10 shadow models; the target model was overfit (65% train vs 48% test).
- Slack et al. 'Fooling LIME and SHAP' (arXiv Feb 2020): a biased classifier can be cloaked to pass LIME/SHAP audits by detecting out-of-distribution perturbed points, demonstrated on COMPAS, German credit, and Communities and Crime.
- Eykholt et al. physical evasion: sticker graffiti on a stop sign caused misclassification in 100% of lab/field tests for one architecture and over 80% for another.
- Goodfellow et al. panda-to-gibbon adversarial example; Beat Buesser's point that merely rotating a lesion image flips a cancer classifier and 'rotating an image is hardly illegal.'
- GLRM cautionary rule: the top MEPS rule (no cancer AND no ADHD diagnosis AND not pregnant predicting low healthcare use) is interpretable yet nonsensical - 'just because a rule can be interpreted, that doesn't necessarily mean that it makes sense.'
- Steinhardt et al.: a 3% data-poisoning rate raised error from 12% to 23% on the IMDb sentiment corpus.
- Mathur et al. 'Dark Patterns at Scale': a crawl of 11,000 shopping sites found 1,818 dark patterns in 15 types grouped into 7 categories (sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, scarcity, obstruction, forced action).
- Legal penalty asymmetry: GDPR max is 4% of global annual turnover or 20 million euro; CCPA is $2,500 per violation ($7,500 if intentional); Illinois BIPA is $1,000 per violation, producing Facebook's $550M Patel v. Facebook settlement.
- Ricci v. DeStefano, 557 U.S. 557 (2009): 20 New Haven firefighters, 5-4 decision under Title VII - the data behind the Black-BoxAuditing 'ricci' example.
- Facebook's content-moderation Oversight Board: 20 members, $130 million funding, opaque selection - the author's example of private 'super-legal' standards undermining democratic legitimacy.
- Tools to remember: IBM AIF360, AIX360, ART; Black-BoxAuditing (BBA); CleverHans (Goodfellow/Papernot, backed by Google AI/OpenAI/Penn State); Foolbox (Bethge Lab, EagerPy); secml; ML Privacy Meter (Shokri).
- Author: Aileen Nielsen, software engineer with a law degree, fellow in law and technology at ETH Zurich; cover animal is the klipspringer, written from the perspective of late 2020.
- Privacy framings cited as primary sources: Solove's Taxonomy of Privacy, Nissenbaum's contextual integrity, Dwork's epsilon-differential privacy, Balkin's information fiduciaries; Zuboff (surveillance capitalism) opens the real-world framing.

## Curriculum fit
For a reader who pairs security research with creativity, leadership, and agentic-AI research, this book is the bridge text between adversarial ML and governance. Security value is direct and high: chapters 9 and 10 are a compact, code-backed primer on membership inference, model inversion/extraction, evasion (HopSkipJump, physical-world attacks), and poisoning/backdoor/clean-label attacks, with working IBM ART, CleverHans, and Foolbox usage and the ML Privacy Meter - this maps cleanly onto an AI-infrastructure attack surface and onto the kind of verification-first, primary-source methodology the reader already runs (the GLRM 'interpretable but wrong' lesson and the 'privacy is not binary' lesson are exactly the scan-produces-candidates, verification-produces-findings distinction). For agentic-AI research it supplies the threat model and the auditing scaffolding (Raji/Google audit framework, FMEA, black-box auditing for indirect influence) that an autonomous evaluator or red-team agent would operationalize, plus the No Free Lunch caution against single-metric trust that applies equally to multi-tool orchestration. For leadership it provides the governance vocabulary - fiduciary duties, dark patterns, value creation, the law-over-markets argument, GDPR/CCPA/BIPA exposure - needed to translate technical findings into business and disclosure terms. The creativity angle is lighter but present in the chaining mindset: fairness as a pipeline of composable interventions and attacks as compositions of boundary-gradient primitives. Caveat for the curriculum: it is a 2020 practitioner book centered on tabular/classification ML and the IBM toolchain, so it predates LLM-specific agentic threats (prompt injection, tool misuse, RAG poisoning) and should be read as the foundational fairness/adversarial-ML layer beneath, not a substitute for, current frontier-model security work.
