## One-liner

A hands-on, Go-based tour of how cryptographic IMPLEMENTATIONS (not the math) get broken, where you write the vulnerable code and the working exploit side by side for every classic attack from LCG cloning to Heartbleed.

## Thesis

Modern cryptographic algorithms are sound; what fails in practice is the implementation. The book's central, repeatedly hammered argument is that the gap between "textbook" cryptography and shipped code is where every real break lives, and that the single most fragile and most catastrophic of those gaps is poor-quality randomness ("random numbers are oxygen to the world of cryptography"). By forcing the reader to write both a deliberately vulnerable implementation (impl_*) and a separate exploit package (exploit_*) that cannot see the secrets, the authors demonstrate that breaking real crypto is a craft of finding implementation tells (reused nonces, unvalidated padding bytes, non-constant-time comparisons, shared primes, leaked internal state) rather than defeating the underlying hard math.

## Core ideas

1. Implementations break, not algorithms: the book's organizing thesis is that the danger lives in the translation of correct 'textbook' math into shipped code; every chapter pairs a deliberately vulnerable impl_ package with a structurally-blind exploit_ package to prove the attacker never needs to defeat the underlying hard problem.
2. Randomness is the load-bearing weakness ('random numbers are oxygen to cryptography'): two full chapters plus the Ch.10 recap argue that poor entropy is the single most recurrent root cause, surfacing again in RSA shared primes (0.3-0.5% of internet keys) and the ECDSA nonce reuse that killed the PS3.
3. Recoverable internal state defeats PRNGs: an LCG (minstd_rand) is cloned by recovering its multiplier/modulus with GCD and modular inverse over math/big, and MT19937 is cloned by 'untempering' 624 consecutive outputs to reconstruct the full 19937-bit state, then predicting all future output.
4. A CSPRNG can be backdoored by design: DUAL_EC_DRBG (NIST SP 800-90A, 2006-2015) is the book's unique example of a cryptographically-secure generator broken intentionally, because whoever knows the secret relation Q = e*P between the two P-256 points can predict all output from ~2 bytes of leaked state.
5. Linearity is fatal for keystream generators: an LFSR's output is a linear function of its state, so capturing known-plaintext keystream lets the attacker solve a linear system (matrix inversion / Berlekamp-Massey) to recover the register and clone it; stream ciphers are just a CSPRNG XORed against plaintext, so key/IV reuse collapses confidentiality.
6. Error messages and padding are an attack surface: the CBC padding oracle decrypts entire ciphertexts using only a true/false 'valid padding?' signal, and the lesson generalizes to 'say decryption failed, not invalid padding' and to timing oracles that leak the same bit through response latency.
7. MAC construction matters more than the hash: secret-prefix MACs fall to length extension (you authenticate without the key by resuming the hash's internal state), secret-suffix MACs fall to collisions, and only HMAC's two-pass ipad/opad construction (or a sponge/SHA-3 hash) resists both.
8. Public-key breaks are RNG breaks in disguise: RSA common-factors (batch GCD over many moduli, Bernstein/Heninger/Lenstra) and Wiener's short-exponent attack (private key recovered as a denominator among the continued-fraction convergents of e/N) both stem from bad key generation rather than from factoring being easy.
9. Signatures are broken by leaking the private key or by sloppy verification: ECDSA's security hinges entirely on a unique random nonce per signature (reuse, or even a few known nonce bits via lattice attacks, yields total key recovery), and Bleichenbacher's e=3 forgery works only because verifiers skip to the NULL separator without checking the FF padding bytes.
10. Time and control flow leak secrets: non-constant-time comparison (glibc memcmp short-circuits), square-and-multiply modular exponentiation (power-trace side channel), and branching/looping on secret values (even via CPU branch prediction and speculative execution) all leak; the fixes are crypto/subtle ConstantTimeCompare, the Montgomery ladder, and branchless CMOV code.
11. Memory and type safety cause two-thirds of all vulnerabilities: Heartbleed (over-read via attacker-controlled payload length feeding memcpy) and the OpenSSH 2004 integer-overflow (nresp * sizeof truncates, loop still runs full count) motivate the CISA/NSA/White-House push toward memory-safe languages, plus mlock, explicit_bzero, and atomic TOCTOU-safe critical sections.
12. Don't roll your own combination of primitives: encryption alone gives confidentiality but not integrity (an MITM can rearrange ciphertext blocks to change a debit amount), EtM risks length extension and MtE risks padding oracles, so use authenticated encryption / AEAD (AES-GCM, CCM) instead of hand-combining a cipher and a MAC.

## Techniques and methods

- LCG cloning: recover minstd_rand's parameters (multiplier 48271, modulus 2^31-1) and seed using GCD and modular inverse over Go's math/big, then reproduce the full sequence.
- MT19937 untempering: invert the Mersenne Twister's tempering transform on 624 consecutive 32-bit outputs to reconstruct the internal state array and predict all subsequent values.
- DUAL_EC_DRBG backdoor demonstration: use crypto/elliptic on P-256 with a chosen Q = e*P so a ~2-byte brute force over the truncated output recovers the next state.
- LFSR state recovery: XOR known plaintext with ciphertext to get keystream, then invert the linear recurrence (linear algebra / Berlekamp-Massey) to clone Fibonacci or Galois LFSRs.
- RC4/WEP FMS attack: collect packets with weak IVs, exploit the 3-5% statistical bias toward key bytes at specific keystream indices, tabulate the most frequent values as key-byte guesses.
- CBC padding-oracle decryption: flip ciphertext bytes in the prior block and read the oracle's valid/invalid-padding response to recover plaintext one byte at a time.
- BEAST: exploit a predictable/reused CBC IV by sliding the unknown secret one byte at a time to a block boundary so each byte is guessed in 2^8 rather than 2^128 tries.
- Rainbow-table construction: build hash chains with reduction functions (Oechslin space-time tradeoff) and crack 6-char alphanumeric password hashes at >20% success, motivating salting plus PBKDF2/Argon2.
- Hash length-extension: set a SHA-256 implementation's internal state to a known digest (the book reaches into OpenSSL via cgo to do this), then append data to forge a valid secret-prefix MAC without the key.
- RSA batch-GCD common-factors attack: run pairwise/batch GCD across a pool of public moduli (the 'chilled RNG' demo: 512-prime pool, 48 RSA-1024 keys, 14 recovered) to find shared primes and factor the keys.
- Wiener's attack: compute the continued-fraction expansion of e/N, walk its convergents, and test each denominator as a candidate private exponent d (works when d < (1/3) * N^(1/4)).
- ECDSA nonce-reuse key recovery: from two signatures sharing r (same nonce k), solve k = (h1 - h2) / (s1 - s2) mod n, then recover d = (k*s - h) / r mod n.
- Bleichenbacher e=3 forgery: build the desired PKCS#1 v1.5 cleartext, find a prefix cube root via bisection search and a suffix cube root via Valsorda's bit-flip method (odd-target trick), and stitch them with garbage hidden in the unvalidated padding region.
- Constant-time and branchless coding: replace memcmp with crypto/subtle ConstantTimeCompare (XOR-accumulate, OR not ADD), replace square-and-multiply with the Montgomery powering ladder, and use CMOV inline assembly to defeat branch-prediction timing leaks.
- Defensive memory/type discipline: avoid strcpy/sprintf/gets, bound memcpy, use uint16_t-style explicit types, mlock secrets, zero with explicit_bzero/OPENSSL_cleanse, and lock resources atomically against TOCTOU.

## Memorable

- 'Random numbers are oxygen to the world of cryptography' - the book's recurring slogan, validated chapter after chapter.
- 0.3% to 0.5% of all RSA keys on the internet shared a prime factor (Lenstra et al. 2012 scanned ~7.6 million keys) - a population-scale failure caused purely by bad randomness, crackable with batch GCD.
- Sony PlayStation 3: secure for ~3 years against the world's best hackers, then totally broken in December 2010 when fail0verflow's segher noticed the r value was IDENTICAL across signatures - Sony reused a fixed ECDSA nonce, leaking the master code-signing private key that 'never left the manufacturing facility', and the hardware-rooted public key could not be revoked on units already sold.
- Heartbleed (OpenSSL 1.0.1f, CVE 2014): a client asks the server to echo a few thousand bytes while supplying only a handful; the unchecked memcpy reads past the buffer and returns adjacent RAM - enough to leak private keys; found independently by Google and Codenomicon via fuzzing.
- The companion repo's discipline: secrets live in impl_* packages with lowercase (unexported) privKey fields so the exploit_* package literally cannot read them in Go - the exploit must work like a real attacker, from public data only.
- DUAL_EC_DRBG was 'the only cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator that was vulnerable by design' - standardized by NIST (SP 800-90A, 2006-2015), backdoored via the relation Q = e*P, and crackable from about 2 bytes of output.
- OpenSSH 2004 integer overflow: nresp = 0x400000ff times sizeof(char*) truncates to allocate only ~1,020 bytes, but the for loop still iterates 0x400000ff times - heap smash to code execution.
- Untempering MT19937 needs exactly 624 consecutive outputs (the state size N=624) to clone the generator and predict everything that follows.
- Rainbow table built in the book cracked >20% of six-character alphanumeric passwords - the concrete payoff that motivates salt + PBKDF2/Argon2.
- Bruce Schneier's closing epigraph - 'Security is a process, not a product' - paired with the authors' own line 'Vigilance is never bought, only practiced.'
- The only known Fermat primes are 3, 5, 17, 257, and 65,537; RSA fixes e to one of these for speed and to keep d large (dodging Wiener) - but e=3 plus lax padding validation opens the door to Bleichenbacher forgery.
- Filippo Valsorda (later Go security lead) simplified the suffix cube-root step of Bleichenbacher's attack in 2016 using the insight that flipping bit i of s flips bit i of s^3 while leaving all lower bits unchanged - but only for odd targets.

## Curriculum fit

Sits squarely between two things students rarely connect: number-theory crypto courses (which prove algorithms secure) and software security courses (which teach exploitation), making it ideal for a junior/senior or early-graduate 'applied cryptography / secure implementation' module, or a hands-on bridge unit in an offensive-security track. Prerequisites are real but modest: comfort reading Go (the entire codebase is Go, with C used only to illustrate memory/type bugs), basic modular arithmetic, and command-line tooling (make, go test). Its strongest pedagogical move - write the vulnerable impl, then write the blind exploit - maps directly onto lab-based courses and CTF-style assessment, and Chapter 10 doubles as a standalone secure-coding checklist (randomness, constant-time, memory/type safety, AEAD, TOCTOU) usable in any AppSec syllabus. Weaknesses for a curriculum: it teaches breaking implementations, not designing protocols or formal proofs, and it assumes a single language, so it complements rather than replaces a theory text like Katz-Lindell or Boneh-Shoup. Best used as the lab/implementation companion to a theory course, or as the spine of a dedicated 'how crypto fails in the real world' elective.
