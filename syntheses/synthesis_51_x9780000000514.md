# 51. Cyberjutsu: Cybersecurity for the Modern Ninja

Ben McCarty's "Cyberjutsu" (No Starch, 2021) is a 26-chapter defensive reader that translates the medieval Japanese ninja scrolls (the Bansenshukai, the Shoninki, the Shinobi Hiden, and the Yoshimori Hyakushu poems, all via Cummins and Minami's translations) into modern cybersecurity practice. The frame is that shinobi were history's first advanced persistent threat (APT0), elite spies-for-hire whose recon-first, deception-heavy tradecraft maps cleanly onto contemporary threat actors. Every chapter follows the same shape: a scroll passage describing an infiltration technique, an analysis of the modern cyber parallel, a "Castle Theory Thought Exercise" (a deliberately un-googleable puzzle where you defend a castle from enemy ninjas, stripped of jargon so any stakeholder can reason about it), a list of "Recommended Security Controls and Mitigations" tagged to specific NIST 800-53 controls, and a Debrief. This is an adversary-mindset-for-defenders book, not a hands-on offensive manual: the payload is how to think, model, and place controls, not exploit code.

The chapter spine, in  terms: Ch 1 Mapping Networks (build the topology/threat-intel map before anything, the recon-first move). Ch 2 Guarding with Special Care (threat modeling, recessed-corner attack vectors). Ch 3 Xenophobic Security (anti-privilege, the argument that interoperability and universal standards ARE the attack surface, build unique characteristics). Ch 4-5 Identification Challenge and Double-Sealed Password (matched-pair authenticators, concealed 2-step auth). Ch 6-7 Hours of Infiltration and Access to Time (time-based controls, anomaly detectors, keeping timing confidential). Ch 8 Tools (living off the land, securing your own tooling). Ch 9 Sensors (the strongest chapter for us: most orgs trust one egress/DMZ sensor, the shinobi answer is many cheap, hidden, inward-facing sensors along predicted enemy paths, plus togiki sensors outside the boundary, passive interfaces as tripwires, and red/purple-team validation of placement). Ch 10 Bridges and Ladders (network boundary bridging). Ch 11 Locks (physical security). Ch 12 Moon on the Water (social engineering as lure). Ch 13 Worm Agent (insider threats, turning an enemy). Ch 14 Ghost on the Moon (implants). Ch 15 The Art of the Fireflies (attribution and counter-attribution: artifacts, strings, and infrastructure can be deliberately planted to push a misattribution narrative, with the three honest options: shed your cognitive biases, build attribution capability, or forget attribution and just defend the behavior). Ch 16 Live Capture (live analysis, deciding whether a target is genuinely inattentive or luring you). Ch 17 Fire Attack (destructive attacks, citing Stuxnet, Shamoon, Industroyer/Crash Override, the Syrian air-defense spoof). Ch 18-19 Covert Communication and Call Signs (C2 detection, marking routes). Ch 20 Light, Noise, and Litter Discipline (OPSEC, the traces an operator leaves, Nmap/Wireshark/China Chopper referenced). Ch 21 Circumstances of Infiltration. Ch 22 Zero-Days (secret exploit knowledge as modern shinobi tradecraft, plus denial-and-deception and the "disconnect" skill of a self-exiled blue team). Ch 23 Hiring Shinobi (the talent-shortage and recruitment problem). Ch 24 Guardhouse Behavior. Ch 25 Zero-Trust Threat Management. Ch 26 Shinobi Tradecraft (the threat-intelligence capstone: Pyramid of Pain, MITRE ATT&CK, Cyber Kill Chain, Diamond Model, STIX, and why the P in TTP stays a mystery).

Two through-lines matter most for our work. First, the ninja philosophy primer (heart under an iron blade, the "correct mind" as mindfulness-as-self-defense, in-nin darkness vs yo-nin plain-sight infiltration) is a clean restatement of the patient-aggregation, restraint, and footprint-awareness ethic  already runs on. Second, the book is relentlessly about communication: McCarty argues most security failures are human and political, and the Castle Theory device exists so a CISO who cannot speak fluent network can still feel and fix the risk, which is our narrative-reporting standard in a different costume.
