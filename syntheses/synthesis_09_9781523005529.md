# Humble Leadership, Second Edition: The Power of Relationships, Openness, and Trust

ISBN: 9781523005529  |  Book #09

## One-liner
A relational re-framing of leadership built on situational humility and "Level 2" whole-person relationships, arguing that real innovation comes from open, trusting groups rather than heroic individuals.

## Thesis
Edgar and Peter Schein define leadership as "the creation and implementation of something new and better" and argue that in a VUCA world no individual can gather enough information to lead alone. Humble Leadership is a practice (not a personality type) that rests on two pillars: situational humility (openness to uncertainty and to what others know) and the deliberate building of Level 2 "personized" relationships of openness and trust. Their core claim is that innovation and growth depend on Level 2 relationships, and that the defining work of a humble leader is to evolve management norms from transactional Level 1 distance toward Level 2 trust without sliding into Level 3 intimacy.

## Structure
### Preface and Part One (Chapters 1-2): The essence of Humble Leadership
Defines leadership as creating something new and better, distinct from management/stewardship which optimize the status quo. Introduces situational humility as a three-part developed skill and the four Levels of Relationship. Argues complex work requires Level 2 whole-person relationships, suggesting leaders shift 20-25 percent of effort from metrics toward collaborative information-sharing.

### Part Two (Chapters 3-5): Examples in different contexts
Six case studies split between founding/building organizations (Singapore under Lee Kwan Yew, DEC under Ken Olsen, the PNHC hospital under fictitious CEO Karl Green) and transforming mature ones (Marquet's submarine, the Swiss chemical firm Multi, the Alpha utility), plus Chapter 5 on group dynamics, T-groups, Lewin's action research, and Saab Combitech's ethnographer exercise. The cases show both Humble Leadership succeeding and regressing to Level 1 under growth pressure.

### Part Three (Chapters 6-8): Culture and the future
Presents the culture model (structure = artifacts/espoused values/underlying assumptions; practice = technical/social/macro) and the waves-on-a-beach metaphor for culture change with headwinds and tailwinds. Two failure cases (Brian's food plant, the BCS startup) show Level 1 defeating Level 2. Chapter 7 introduces meta culture and six future trends including AI; Chapter 8 is an allegory of a startup decaying from Level 2.5 to Level Minus 1 through bureaucratization.

### Part Four (Chapters 9-10): Experiential learning
Hands-on exercises: mindful reflection on past Level 2 relationships, a nine-step relationship map putting yourself at the center and labeling lines by relationship level, the lemons exercise for learning to see uniquely rather than categorically, ground-up relationship building, and the eight ways groups make decisions with their traps.

### Back matter: References, Acknowledgments, Index, Author bios
Extensive references anchoring the work to McGregor, Lewin, Edmondson, Marquet, Sahlins, Kahneman, Sutton, and the Scheins' own series. Acknowledgments trace the lineage to Douglas McGregor's 1956 mentorship. Notes Ed Schein died in January 2023 at age 94 shortly after completing the manuscript.

## Core ideas
- Leadership is defined narrowly as 'the creation and implementation of something new and better,' deliberately separated from management, administration, stewardship, and governance, which optimize the present rather than create change.
- Situational humility is a developed skill, not a trait: accepting uncertainty while staying curious, being mindfully open to what others know, and recognizing when unconscious biases distort perception.
- The four Levels of Relationship: Level Minus 1 (negative/coercive, sentiment antipathy), Level 1 (transactional/role-based, apathy), Level 2 (whole-person/personized, empathy), Level 3 (intimate, compassion), plus an intermediate Level 2.5.
- 'Personizing' (the authors' coined term, distinct from personalization) means accepting and seeing the whole person to build psychological safety; personalization may feel real but is inauthentic and does not sustain safety.
- Level 2 does not require friendship, niceness, or sharing private life; it requires openness and trust around work: keeping commitments, not undermining or sabotaging, not lying or withholding task-relevant information.
- The level of relationship should match the work: simple, clear-role tasks tolerate Level 1, but the more complex, collaborative, and undefined the roles, the more Level 2 becomes essential.
- Organizations are socio-technical systems; leaders are seduced by managing the technical 'dials' (metrics, KPIs) and starve the social/collaborative process, missing information held by people without formal authority.
- Culture is 'accumulated shared learning' and has two faces: structure (artifacts, espoused values, underlying assumptions as conventions) and practice (technical, social, macro layers as here-and-now intentions). Social culture is far stickier than technical culture.
- Culture change is nonlinear, modeled as waves on a beach: leadership is the cresting wave, culture is the sediment, onshore wind is the tailwind of change and offshore wind the headwind of resistance; change comes through repeated wash and backwash, not linear cause-effect.
- Transparency is not the same as openness: transparency is passive display of metrics and can become a weapon (the BCS case), while openness is an active choice to reveal, listen, and respond around what matters for the task.
- Growth and resource scarcity exert a gravitational pull toward bureaucratization and regression from Level 2 down through Level 1 to Level Minus 1, illustrated by both DEC's silo collapse and the Chapter 8 allegory.
- In an AI-augmented future, content and expertise lose value (the 'leveling' effect) while context, process, and Level 2 relationships gain value; AI handles known unknowns, but only Level 2 relationships let groups muddle through unknown unknowns.
- Hierarchy and Level 2 are compatible: Marquet's submarine and Multi's rotating board show that empowerment and whole-person relationships can coexist with formal rank, often by rotating leaders through roles so everyone knows each other's jobs.
- Leadership is a 'group sport' that can emerge from any group member, not just the designated leader; it rotates with the task and lives in a mosaic of relationships rather than a top-down status.

## Techniques and methods
- Situational humility as a 3-point skill: (1) accept uncertainty while staying curious, (2) be mindfully open to what others observe, (3) recognize bias-driven distortion and emotional triggers.
- The Four Levels of Relationship taxonomy (Exhibit 2.1) plus the Sentiments mapping (Exhibit 2.2): antipathy / apathy / empathy / compassion as a 'gut check' to tell Level 1 from Level 2.
- Culture Structure model (Exhibit 6.1): Artifacts (seen/heard/felt) over Espoused values (what insiders say) over Underlying assumptions (nonnegotiable core beliefs), first introduced in Schein 1985.
- Culture Practice model (Exhibit 6.2): Technical (strategy/mission/design), Social (communication and relationship norms), Macro (global/occupational/cohort/tech/enviro-socio-political trends), drawing on Sahlins' structure-and-practice continuum.
- Waves-on-a-beach generative metaphor for culture change: wave = leadership crest, beach/sediment = culture, onshore wind = change tailwind, offshore wind = resistance headwind, wash/backwash = change and organizational response.
- Meta culture scenario planning: brainstorm future cultural trends (Gibson's 'the future is already here, just not evenly distributed') and classify them as tailwinds or headwinds for a change initiative.
- Marquet's 'leader-leader' language reform: replace 'Request permission to / I would like to / What should I do about' with 'I intend to / I plan to / I will,' preceded by stating the why for complex decisions.
- Relationship map exercise (9 steps): self in center circle, circles for work/family/lost/future relationships positioned by status, lines weighted by intensity then labeled by level (L-1, L1, L2, L2.5, L3) to plan which relationships to deepen.
- The lemons exercise: each participant studies, names, introduces, and re-finds their own lemon to demonstrate that categorical observation fails where attention to unique detail succeeds, a primer for personizing.
- Eight ways groups make decisions (Exhibit 10.1, from 1956 NTL workshops): the plop, self-authorization, minority rule, discuss-and-vote, voting, polling, consensus testing, unanimous agreement, with named traps (silence is not agreement, voting creates undermining minorities, status suppresses juniors).
- Ground-up relationship building protocol: choose to ask a more personal question or reveal something personal, keep it work-relevant (avoid favorite-animal icebreakers), ask narrative-eliciting questions you don't know the answer to, then run a 'process analysis' on what worked.
- The Toyota Production System / Lean 'stop the line' practice adapted to a hospital patient-safety alert system (PNHC case) so any team member can halt treatment and trigger immediate review.

## Memorable
- 'Leadership Is the Creation and Implementation of Something New and Better' (the book's working definition).
- Ken Olsen of DEC, asked why he didn't decide himself: 'First of all, I am not that smart. I also learned once, when I made a decision and started to walk down the road... I discovered that there was no one behind me.'
- A US Army two-star general told the authors that in training 'Our guys go from Level Minus 1 to Level 4' (the model only goes to Level 3).
- Marquet: 'Like so many times, my not knowing the answer ahead of time helped me. Instead of a scripted meeting where I pretended to solicit ideas, we had an honest conversation' (Turn the Ship Around, p. 170).
- Robert Sutton's rule cited as 'bad behavior is five times more powerful than good behavior,' implying positive trust acts must significantly outnumber negative ones.
- William Gibson: 'The future is already here, it's just not evenly distributed' (used to motivate meta culture planning).
- Thomas Friedman's reframe that AI is really 'IA' (intelligent assistance), not the end of jobs.
- The authors' recurring slogan: 'Leadership is a group sport.'
- Rule of thumb: allocate 20 to 25 percent of work effort away from managing metrics toward collaborative, co-creative information sharing.
- INPO task forces: about half a day to spot critical trust/openness problems at a nuclear plant, but two weeks to build enough relationship to address them.
- The Alpha eye-shield case: an employee fired for not wearing his eye shield was reinstated and put on a task force after the committee learned his shield had fogged in extreme humidity, turning blame culture into systemic fix.
- Sun Microsystems espoused 'We're a systems company' while its artifacts (servers, storage, microelectronics) made it primarily a hardware company, the book's example of artifact/espoused-value mismatch.
- The 'TMI' (too much information) colloquialism cited as a signal of crossing the Level 2 to Level 3 boundary.
- The orange construction-cone 'do not disturb' signal at BCS, a clever but largely ignored fix showing social culture won't shift via a simple trick.

## Curriculum fit
For an agentic-AI researcher, the strongest payload is the relationship-levels framework as an interface vocabulary for multi-agent and human-agent systems: the orchestrator-subagent task-spec handoff is essentially a Level 1 to Level 2 question (role-and-rule transaction versus open, trust-bearing information flow), and the book's claim that complex tasks fail at Level 1 maps directly onto why poorly specified subagent handoffs degrade. The eight decision-making methods and their traps (silence is not agreement, voting creates undermining minorities) are a concrete model for designing agent voting/consensus and for understanding LLM-judge ensembles. For creativity and leadership, situational humility, the structure-versus-practice culture model, and the waves-on-a-beach change metaphor are durable mental tools, and the 'context over content' future-trend argument about AI leveling expertise is a sharp lens for an independent researcher whose edge is method and chaining rather than gatekept information. For security research the transfer is indirect but real: the Schein theme that information needed for good decisions is widely distributed and that people withhold bad news from bosses who shoot the messenger parallels the verification-stage discipline (the data lives below the role/status layer; the un-asked tech or custodian sees the finding). Weakest fit is anything quantitative or adversarial; this is a soft-skills, case-narrative book with one chart and no formulas, so use it for framing and team/agent-design intuition, not technique.
