# Becoming Resilient: Staying Connected Under Adversity

ISBN: 9781637424438  |  Book #29

## One-liner
A non-technical-leadership-meets-systems-engineering argument that "resiliency" is a distinct discipline from security, fault tolerance, and disaster recovery, and that civilians and businesses should adopt the U.S. military's damaged-warship mindset (minimum-essential mode, collaborative sharing, rapid reconstitution) to keep critical digital systems running through compound cyber-plus-natural disasters.

## Thesis
Schutzer argues that our total dependence on digital infrastructure has outpaced our ability to defend it, so the old posture of detect-prevent-protect is no longer enough: attacks and natural disasters cannot be fully stopped, will increasingly co-occur, and will take systems down for days to months. The remedy is resiliency, a distinct discipline whose threat model assumes a sophisticated, persistent, adaptive adversary that gets in and stays in, and whose goal is to keep prioritized mission-critical functions running in a degraded "minimum essential" mode while rapidly reconstituting full service. The central operating model is borrowed from warfighting (seal the flooded compartment, fight with what survives, get help from neighboring ships, innovate workarounds) and the central civilian mechanism is trusted collaborative sharing of communications, compute, and power among neighbors, businesses, and government, hardened against attack and backed by a funded research and measurement agenda.

## Structure
### Front matter, Description, and Testimonial
Frames the book: catastrophic compound events (natural disaster plus physical plus cyber, possibly simultaneous and rehearsed) are growing in frequency and lethality, while 5G/6G/Wi-Fi 6 and LEO satellites deepen dependence. Salvatore Stolfo's testimonial sharpens the thesis: resiliency must be a 'first-class principle in the design of every layer of the communications stack' and is NOT the same as security, fault tolerance, or business continuity. Keywords listed: resiliency, survivability, rapid reconstitution, systems recovery, adaptability, cybersecurity, cyber warfare, environmental disaster, system availability.

### Chapter 1 - Communications and Other Enabling Technologies
A long technology survey establishing both the dependency problem and the resiliency toolkit. Covers the OSI seven-layer and TCP/IP four-layer models, the Internet hourglass model (a distinguished narrow-waist layer enabling layer-independent substitution), then physical-layer advances: 5G (10-20 Gbps, 1 ms latency, 99.999% availability, NFV/SDN), 6G (terahertz, ~1000 Gbps, ~100 billion base stations, edge devices as antennas), Wi-Fi 6/6E/7 (BSS coloring, MU-MIMO, OFDMA, TWT, MLO, 4K-QAM), Wi-Fi vs mobile tradeoffs, satellites (LEO/MEO/GEO, Van Allen belts, Starlink and its Ukraine reconstitution), and drones (HAWK30, Qualcomm RB5, AERPAW). Then enabling tech: AI/ML, cryptography (symmetric/asymmetric/hash, salting), quantum threat and Shor's algorithm, NIST PQC winners, QKD, FIDO passkeys, edge/cloud, blockchain/crypto caveats, digital twins, low-code, IoT. Closes on communications diversity as the foundation of resiliency and the ISP-concentration problem.

### Chapter 2 - 21st-Century Trends and Their Impact on Our Daily Lives
Sector-by-sector demonstration that digital dependence is now load-bearing across health care (telemedicine, FDA-cleared insulin-pump app, implantables, 6G BCIs and the liability wave), education (Khan Academy, flipped/station-rotation/blended learning, education savings accounts), entertainment and social (streaming churn, shoppable media, metaverse, NFTs), commerce (e-commerce growth, voice assistants, drone delivery), transportation (EVs, autonomy, AAVs, Hyperloop, V2X), power (grid resilience, ESG, distributed solar/battery, sabotage), and business/finance/future-of-work (composable orgs, gig workforce, cloud migration). A substantial '21st-Century Battlefield' section then argues civilians must learn from the military, citing Sun Tzu, Unrestricted Warfare, Richard Clarke's Cyber War, USMC MCDP-8, and Zelensky's Starlink use as a 'systems overmatch' example. Ends on government as funder/regulator and ransomware victim.

### Chapter 3 - The Growing Threat
Catalogs the threat surface: global warming Gray Rhino disasters (solar coronal mass ejections, transformer cascades), the asymmetric attacker advantage (defender must cover all vulns, attacker needs one), attribution difficulty, and the anatomy of ransomware (infiltration, privilege escalation, lateral movement, C2, double extortion, RaaS). Names the gangs (Conti, REvil, LockBit, Pysa, DoppelPaymer, Maze/ChaCha, Wizard Spider, DarkSide) and incidents (Colonial Pipeline, Ireland health system, HBO, Costa Rica). Covers cryptocurrency tracing, Mt. Gox, Dark/Deep Web and Tor, DEF CON, and zero-days. Argues compound simultaneous events plus rising geopolitical tension make extended outages inevitable.

### Chapter 4 - What Is Resilience, and How Is It Measured and Tested?
The definitional core. Aggregates resiliency definitions from NIST, NIAC, OMB, DoD, DHS Risk Lexicon, Sterbenz, and Madni/Jackson, then carefully distinguishes resiliency from security, disaster recovery / business continuity / COOP, and fault tolerance by its threat model: long dwell time, APT, supply-chain contamination, adversary adaptation, reconstitution-while-under-attack. Resiliency = anticipate, withstand, recover, evolve, plus prioritization of a minimum-essential objective set. Discusses measurement difficulty (MOEs, MOPs, KPPs, Red Team plus analytic methods, deception dwell-time and segmentation as measurable proxies), FEMA National Risk Index (18 hazards), and government investments. Concludes many needed primitives already exist (encryption, anti-jamming, backup) so action should start now even before metrics mature.

### Chapter 5 - A Blueprint for Becoming More Resilient
The prescriptive chapter. Three backup approaches: in-house remote sites, third-party cloud / DRaaS (pay-on-use), and collaborative sharing among trusted neighbors and businesses. Heavy emphasis on trusted collaborative sharing of Wi-Fi/cellular/compute/power as the affordable resiliency mechanism, with prioritization of scarce bandwidth, identity verification, zero-trust schemes, and protection of coordination channels (encryption, steganography, low-probability-of-intercept out-of-band). The warship analogy is made explicit. Lays out a four-track government-funded research agenda: (1) intel collection/sharing, (2) sharing compute/comms/power and rapid reconstitution, (3) protecting and mutating sensitive info, (4) education. Names candidate technologies: smart object databases, DRM-style embedded data, decoy/fake data co-existing with real, drones/LEO, mobile power, AI asset mapping, secret backup systems, quantum comms.

### Chapter 6 - Concluding Remarks
Synthesis and call to action. Restates that the digital revolution has 'won,' dependence is total, and the threat (cyber plus climate plus geopolitical) has outgrown legacy backup/recovery. Reiterates the warship model and the defense spectrum 'defer, protect, detect, adapt, recover' with new emphasis on adapt and recover and on collaboration ('United we stand, divided we fall'). Stresses culture: composable architecture, decentralized decision-making delegated to those closest to systems, entrepreneurial mindset, relationships over pure tooling. Gives a five-component resilience framework (identify mission-critical needs, protect, detect, respond, sharing-and-recovery plan), notes mean malware dwell time ~200 days, and ends on the aspirational 'ultimate capability': periodically practice rebuilding everything from scratch so resilience is 'baked in.'

## Core ideas
- Resiliency is a separate discipline, not a synonym for security: security assumes the adversary is kept out or quickly removed and outages are brief; resiliency assumes a sophisticated adversary defeats boundary/IdAM/IDR controls, dwells long, and you must keep operating and reconstitute WHILE still under attack.
- The threat model is the differentiator. Resiliency's threat model explicitly includes supply-chain contamination, long adversary dwell times (cited as up to ~200 days to detect malware), APT persistence, and loss of control over part of the resource set, none of which physical/geographic duplicate-site separation can mitigate.
- Compound, simultaneous, rehearsed events are the planning baseline: cyberattacks can be deliberately launched during a hurricane or pandemic for maximum impact, so plan for cyber-plus-natural-disaster co-occurrence, not isolated single events.
- Minimum-essential mode plus prioritization is the operational heart: when you can no longer do everything, you must already know the minimum set of mission-critical objectives to keep running and what can be dropped, and how to allocate scarce surviving bandwidth/compute/power against that priority list.
- The warfighting analogy is the book's organizing metaphor: a hit warship seals flooded compartments, fights with surviving resources, gets help from neighboring ships/aircraft, and reconstitutes with innovative workarounds; civilians should import this mindset, training, and culture.
- Trusted collaborative sharing is the affordable civilian resiliency mechanism: neighbors and businesses share surviving Wi-Fi/cellular/compute/power in a daisy-chain of trust, ideally pre-arranged, requiring identity verification and prioritization, because individual redundant sites are too costly for SMBs and individuals.
- Sharing itself becomes an attack surface. As reconstitution and sharing become common they attract hackers (DoS, interception, resource compromise, data theft, blackmail), so coordination channels need encryption, steganography, low-probability-of-intercept out-of-band comms, and strong mutual authentication from scratch.
- Layered architecture and the Internet hourglass model enable resiliency by design: a narrow distinguished waist lets you swap a severed wired link for a rapidly deployed wireless/satellite/drone link, or default a degraded network to a safer architecture (like an OS booting into safe mode), without breaking the apps above.
- Communications diversity is the foundation: maintaining multiple independent paths (wired plus cellular plus Wi-Fi plus satellite plus drone, increasingly self-powered with solar/batteries) raises the odds at least one path survives any single failure.
- Resiliency cannot be bought as a product; it is a culture, plan, and trained capability: composable (interchangeable building-block) architecture, decentralized decision-making delegated to those closest to the systems, an entrepreneurial mindset, and relationships that are 'far harder to imitate than purely automated software-based solutions.'
- Measurement is the unsolved problem and the reason resiliency lags: we lack good metrics for cost-effectiveness of resiliency investments against an adaptive adversary, so assessments are qualitative/semi-quantitative, but the author insists we must invest now rather than wait for mature metrics.
- The ultimate capability is the ability to rebuild from scratch, practiced as a habit: periodically relearning to rebuild everything from zero both proves and improves resilience and is 'resilience baked in.'
- Recovery must not reopen the door: because threat actors plant backdoors to return, cleanup and restoration must be done in a way that prevents the attacker's easy re-entry; a clean validated backup is the recovery lifeline, and if it fails you need alternatives.
- Government is a multi-front enabler: funding research, providing emergency comms-as-a-service (San Francisco, New York free Wi-Fi), risk information tools (FEMA National Risk Index, NOAA maps), supply-chain resilience via reshoring/friend-shoring loans and subsidies, and public-private intelligence sharing.

## Techniques and methods
- The defense spectrum: defer, protect, detect, adapt, recover, with the book's argument to add weight to 'adapt and recover' and especially recovery-through-collaboration.
- Five-component resilience mindset/framework: (1) identify mission-critical needs via critical asset and process mapping plus risk-and-readiness assessment, (2) protect with traditional first-line defenses, (3) detect via security analytics and continuous watch, (4) respond with the right tools for security and physical failures, (5) sharing-and-recovery plan with coordinated get-everything-back-on-track mechanisms.
- Four-track government research agenda: (1) collaboration for intelligence collection/sharing; (2) collaboration for sharing processing/comms/power and rapid reconstitution; (3) collaboration for protecting/using/mutating sensitive info; (4) education about resiliency.
- Resiliency assessment methodology: combine retrospective operational analysis, Red Team emulation/test in limited environments, and analytic measurement (broader attack coverage, less realism), reporting MOEs, MOPs, and KPPs covering how quickly, how completely, and with how much confidence functions can be reconstituted.
- Resiliency proxy metrics: adversary dwell time in a deception environment as a measure of deception effectiveness; time between compromise of one enclave/segment and a similar resource in another enclave as a measure of segmentation effectiveness.
- Minimum-essential operations mode: prioritize the mission-critical objective subset, isolate from damaged/corrupted components, take stock of surviving functioning components/connections, then reallocate and adapt remaining resources against agreed priorities.
- Hardened collaborative-sharing protocol: verify identity of sharing parties (in-person/informal for neighbors, established procedures for banks/merchants, physical tokens exchanged in advance), prioritize and allocate scarce resources by need/criticality, and protect coordination with encryption, steganography, and out-of-band low-probability-of-intercept channels.
- Bootstrap/from-scratch reconstitution kit: secure space with emergency equipment and startup software/protocols (possibly mobile), computers with startup code and built-in trusted checkpoints (possibly blockchain-backed), emergency power (solar/generators/batteries), emergency stealth startup comms (drones, LEO, antennas), startup crypto codes to establish secure channels from scratch, and one-time startup access codes for mutual authentication.
- Internet hourglass / layered substitution technique: swap technologies at a single layer (4G-to-5G, wired-to-wireless link, advanced routing) without disrupting layers above; a degraded residual network can default to a safer architecture (e.g., SCION isolation domains) like an OS in safe mode.
- Candidate resiliency technologies named as a toolbox: smart object-oriented databases with preapproved authenticated access and alerting, DRM-style embedding of sensitive data inside programs accessible only to trusted parties, databases where real and purposely-false data co-exist (needing 2+ collaborating partners to discern), AI asset mapping and credential-compromise detection, digital twins for vulnerability simulation, low-code for non-programmer emergency software, quantum comms/QKD for from-scratch channel setup.
- Backup selection logic: in-house remote site (expensive, SMB-unaffordable) vs third-party cloud vs DRaaS (pay-per-use standby capacity), with synchronization done to avoid replicating malware/corruption into the backup and a bootstrap plan for unsynced gaps.
- Practice discipline: document incident response plans and run internal response / critical-system-isolation exercises at least annually to 'exponentially' improve response time; aspire to periodically rebuild from scratch.

## Curriculum fit
"This is a strategy/leadership-and-systems-thinking book, not a technical how-to, and it earns a place on the list precisely because it reframes the security-research mental model. For a security researcher it is most valuable as a conceptual lens: the rigorous separation of resiliency from security, fault tolerance, and disaster recovery (Chapter 4) gives precise vocabulary for the difference between 'we blocked the attack' and 'we kept the mission running while breached and reconstituted while under fire' - directly relevant to how findings are framed in terms of mission impact and time-to-reconstitute rather than CVE counts. Its threat-model emphasis (long dwell time, supply-chain contamination, adaptive adversary, recover-while-attacked, backdoors that reopen the door) maps cleanly onto adversary-emulation and post-exploitation thinking, and the ransomware-anatomy and gang taxonomy in Chapter 3 are a usable, if dated (2022-2023), reference. On leadership and creativity it is strongest: the warfighting-mindset chapters, the composable-organization and decentralized-decision-making argument, the 'delegate decisions to those closest to the system,' the entrepreneurial-mindset-over-tooling claim, and the 'practice rebuilding from scratch' habit are genuinely useful organizational-resilience and team-design ideas. For agentic-AI research the fit is thinner and mostly thematic: AI appears as a tool for asset mapping, anomaly detection, corruption recovery, and credential-compromise detection, and the book repeatedly notes AI as a double-edged adversary capability, but it offers no agent architecture or technical depth. The technology survey (5G/6G/Wi-Fi 7/satellite/PQC/QKD) is broad-but-shallow and somewhat time-stamped, so treat it as orientation, not authority. Net: read it for the resiliency-vs-security distinction, the minimum-essential-mode and collaborative-sharing operating models, and the leadership/culture material; skip-skim the per-sector trend chapters and the comms-tech survey."
