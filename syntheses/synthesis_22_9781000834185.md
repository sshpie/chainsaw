# The Power of Humility in Leadership: Influencing as a Role Model

ISBN: 9781000834185  |  Book #22

## One-liner
A research-grounded leadership book by Franziska Frank arguing that humility, defined as four trainable behaviors rather than self-abasement, measurably outperforms ego-driven leadership, backed by 160-plus executive interviews, a 10-country survey, and hundreds of cited studies.

## Thesis
Frank argues that humility is not weakness or self-deprecation but a strength-based virtue with four components: accurate self-assessment (showing both strengths and weaknesses), genuine appreciation of others, openness to learning and feedback, and seeing the bigger picture (self-transcendence). She marshals empirical evidence that humble leadership drives better performance, retention, creativity, psychological safety, and even stock returns, while narcissistic leadership is costly. The central practical claim is that humility, like charisma, IQ-adjacent skills, and even personality, can be deliberately learned and trained at any age, in any culture, and that the leader's own modeled behavior is the primary lever for instilling it in an organization.

## Structure
### Front matter, Introduction, Chapters 1-3 (Image, Definition/Measurement, Status in Companies)
Establishes the problem: 'humility' is semantically loaded (humus/earth vs humiliation), so managers reject the word while wanting the substance. Frank defines humility operationally via four elements and measures it using Owens' Expressed Humility Scale (Cronbach Alpha 0.92-0.96) plus three bigger-picture items from Ou. Her data show a large gap: ~97 percent of employees want a humble leader but only ~35 percent worldwide get one; roughly a third of managers are seen as narcissistic. Seeing the bigger picture scores worst of the four elements.

### Chapter 4 (The Benefits of Humility)
Catalogs measured upsides: humble CEOs correlate with better firm innovation and performance, lower turnover, higher psychological safety (links to Edmondson and Google's Project Aristotle), better customer loyalty, more creativity, and stronger ethics. Investors initially lower expectations for humble CEOs, who then beat them, helping stock performance. Connects to Collins' Level 5 Leadership (personal humility plus professional will) and the ambidextrous (exploit/explore) organization. Reviews CEO-impact debate (5 vs 40 percent, settling near 20).

### Chapter 5 (Showing Strengths and Weaknesses Correctly) and Chapter 6 (Appreciating Others)
Ch5: accurate self-view requires showing both strengths and weaknesses when it serves the bigger picture; covers Dunning-Kruger, imposter syndrome, spotlight effect, overconfidence, narcissism (DSM-5, grandiose vs vulnerable, hypo-egoic non-entitlement), hedonic editing for bundling bad news, fake-weakness humble-bragging (humble-braggers rated 3.03 vs 3.93 hireability), and asking-for-advice research. Ch6: appreciation doubles motivation (31 to 67 percent), drives the Pygmalion effect (and warns of the golem effect), and is hard because humans poorly perceive others (Simons inattention video, Ekman micro-expressions, fundamental attribution error, set-up-to-fail syndrome). Introduces BET (Behavior-Effect-Thanks) and BEAR (Behavior-Effect-Alternative-Result) feedback models, Netflix 4A feedback, and the Gottman 5:1 positivity ratio (work version 3:1, ~80 percent positive).

### Chapter 7 (Ability to Learn and Openness) and Chapter 8 (Seeing the Bigger Picture)
Ch7: nearly everything leadership needs can be learned, even charisma (Antonakis' 12 tactics, 9 verbal/3 non-verbal, taught in a 5-hour intervention). Covers Dweck's growth vs fixed mindset (questionnaire scored 0-54), neuroplasticity, Flynn effect, IQ-leadership curvilinear gap (best at +18 IQ points over employees), priming, intellectual humility, mindfulness as a learning tool (Toto Wolff/Mercedes F1, Rockwell Fish Bowl), and coaching (effect ~55 percent after four sessions). Ch8: the bigger picture means accepting finitude, replaceability, the large role of luck, and self-transcendence; warns against victim thinking, teaches frugal innovation through constraints, and uses awe (small-self effect) plus reflective questions (ex-ante-ex-post, pre-mortem) to keep perspective.

### Chapter 9 (Limits and Disadvantages) and Chapter 10 (Nationalities, Age, Gender)
Ch9: a rigorous self-critique. Humility must be authentic (fake humility breeds distrust and emotional exhaustion via surface acting); it depends on competence, top-team health (faultlines), power and power-distance; it can let employees object, cause cozy under-sharing, stress over-trusted employees, and create a less-agentic image. Conclusion: no study shows correctly-lived humility is seriously damaging. Ch10: a 10-country, 100-employee-each survey (Britain, China, France, Germany, India, Italy, Netherlands, Singapore, Switzerland, US). India scores highest (~53 percent humble across all 12 behaviors), China lowest (~16 percent), Germany worst at seeking critical feedback. Humility works everywhere but must be expressed differently per culture. Gender and age differences are minor; narcissism is costlier to male than female leaders.

### Chapter 11 (Politics), Chapter 12 (Examples), Chapter 13 (Implementation/Future), Appendix A (FAQs), Index
Ch11: voters claim to want humble politicians (86 percent) but often pick narcissists; covers Owen's 14-point Hubris Syndrome, attachment styles, social dominance orientation, self-esteem effects, and humble exemplars (Mandela, Merkel, Ardern, Obama, Mujica, Ford). Ch12: case studies of Pixar Braintrust, Zappos shuttle-driver test, Benjamin Franklin, a Benedictine monk's 12 humility sentences, and quotes from Cook, Nadella, Buffett, Hastings, Werner, Rossmann, Fiorina. Ch13: three-step implementation (inventory, interventions, model-it-yourself) and a Teal/Laloux-inspired future. Appendix: humility is a virtue underpinning all leadership styles, not a style itself; distinguished from servant leadership; sub-types are intellectual and cultural humility.

## Core ideas
- Humility is defined operationally as four trainable behaviors, not a personality trait or self-abasement: accurate self-assessment (showing strengths AND weaknesses when it serves the bigger picture), appreciating others' contributions, openness to learning and feedback, and seeing the bigger picture (self-transcendence). The HUMBLE acronym encodes them: Unveil themselves, Measure others positively, Believe in openness, Look to the bigger picture, End rule of ego.
- There is a persistent perception gap: over 80 percent of people believe they are already humble, yet employees see only about a third of managers as humble. People rate themselves on average 46 percent humbler than their own direct leaders (a measured hubris).
- Humility is a strength that produces hard business outcomes (innovation, retention, customer loyalty, creativity, ethics, even stock performance), directly countering the assumption that it signals weakness; Frank repeatedly anchors this in research rather than assertion.
- Nearly everything a leader needs can be learned, including charisma (Antonakis' 12 tactics taught in a 5-hour session moved managers from 2.45 to 3.00 on a 5-point charisma scale), emotional intelligence, leadership style, and even Big Five personality traits when the person genuinely wants to change.
- Carol Dweck's growth-vs-fixed mindset is load-bearing: belief that ability is trainable (neuroplasticity, brain-as-muscle) materially improves outcomes; one intervention lifted goal-attainment 10 percent overall and 20 percent in mathematics. Praise effort and practice, never innate talent.
- Appreciation is high-leverage and easy to get wrong: feeling appreciated roughly doubles self-motivation (31 to 67 percent), but humans are bad at perceiving others (Simons inattention video, Ekman micro-expressions, fundamental attribution error). Real appreciation must be specific and tied to a larger meaning, not generic ('Great presentation' vs 'slide 9 made the implementation gap clearer').
- The Pygmalion effect (high expectations make people perform better, working subconsciously) and its inverse the golem effect mean a leader's belief in people literally shapes their output; humility unlocks the goodwill that powers Pygmalion.
- Seeing the bigger picture requires accepting finitude, replaceability, and the dominant role of luck, plus refusing victimhood; constraints actually boost creativity ('frugal innovation', Dr. Seuss writing Green Eggs and Ham on a 50-word bet, Renault's 6,000-dollar car).
- Fake humility is worthless and harmful: employees detect it, withhold trust, and the faking leader suffers emotional exhaustion through surface acting; humble-bragging backfires measurably. Authenticity, especially genuine active listening, is the discriminator.
- Humility has real boundaries Frank refuses to hide: it depends on competence, top-team health (faultlines), the leader actually having power, and on power-distance and demographic similarity; over-trusted employees can be stressed and humble managers can be perceived as less agentic.
- Humility works across all 10 surveyed countries but must be expressed differently per culture (e.g., Japan needs prepared rather than ad-hoc feedback per Netflix's No Rules); the concept is universal even where the word is semantically hostile.
- Gender and age produce only small, mostly statistically irrelevant differences; importantly, narcissism is costlier for male than female leaders (humble Hester and Henry both seen as competent by ~88 percent, but narcissistic Nick rated competent by only 53 vs Nina's 67).
- In politics humility is harder: voters say they want it (86 percent) but gravitate to narcissists, and structural pressures (party, press, hubris) push the other way; Owen's 14-point Hubris Syndrome is an acquired, office-induced disorder distinct from childhood narcissism.
- Humility is best understood as a virtue underlying every leadership style (the 'wardrobe' of styles), not a style itself; this is why it also applies to employees, generating a shared organizational language that servant leadership cannot.
- Implementation is a three-step program (honest inventory, awareness/behavioral interventions, then modeling humility yourself as the primary lever) and the leader's role-model behavior matters every minute of every working day.

## Techniques and methods
- The four elements of humility framework and the HUMBLE acronym (Unveil, Measure others positively, Believe in openness, Look to bigger picture, End rule of ego)
- BET model for positive feedback: Behavior, Effect, Thanks
- BEAR model for critical feedback: Behavior, Effect, Alternative, Result
- Netflix 4A feedback rules: Aim to assist, Actionable, Appreciate the feedback, Accept-or-discard; plus Live 360 dinners
- Antonakis' 12 charismatic leadership tactics (9 verbal: metaphors, stories, contrasts, rhetorical questions, three-part lists, moral conviction, sharing collective feelings, high expectations, conviction expectations can be met; 3 non-verbal: animated voice, active facial expression, gestures)
- Owens Expressed Humility Scale supplemented by Ou's bigger-picture items as the measurement instrument
- Dweck-derived growth/fixed mindset questionnaire (Tables 7.1/7.2, scored 0-54: 43-54 strong growth down to 0-18 strong fixed)
- Mindfulness practices for perception and ego-quieting: breath focus, open awareness, conscious eating, conscious everyday action; the 'internal balcony'
- Awe induction to produce the 'small self' and increase humility (nature video/VR, galaxy screensaver, earth-from-space images)
- Reflective bigger-picture questions: the imagined 80-year-old self (ex-ante-ex-post), the 2030 pre-mortem, 'whom do I belong to', gratitude-and-protect
- Pygmalion-activating language: 'I'm sure you can do this because of your X' plus 'Do you see it that way too?' to create commitment; the green-circle-with-three-red-lines feedback image
- Empowering-question move (Steve Jobs/Corning gorilla glass 'You can do it'; Goetz Werner's director-to-evocator: answer questions with three counter-questions)
- The mirror test and the 'What does THIS employee/company need now' (not 'what would I want') reframing borrowed from good-parenting
- Self-labeling technique ('I don't' vs 'I can't') and labeling others to shift behavior (smoker willpower study)
- Frugal innovation: deliberately imposing constraints to raise creativity and defeat victim thinking
- Three-step organizational implementation: honest humility inventory, awareness/behavioral interventions, leader self-modeling; plus the Trojan-horse tactic of avoiding the loaded word 'humility' early (use 'modern and mature leadership' or 'timely feedback')
- Owen's 14-point Hubris Syndrome checklist for diagnosing office-acquired hubris in leaders
- Goleman's six leadership styles (coercive, authoritative/visionary, democratic, affiliative, pacesetting, coaching) framed as a situational 'wardrobe'

## Memorable
- Roughly 97 percent of employees want a humble leader but only about 35 percent worldwide get one; on the 12-behavior measure, 53 percent (India) is the highest any country reaches and China is lowest at 16 percent.
- Appreciation roughly doubles self-motivation: 31 percent without it, 67 percent with it (Harter and Adkins / Gallup).
- Humble-braggers were rated only 3.03 on hireability versus 3.93 for genuine answers; 77 percent gave humble-brags vs 23 percent genuine weaknesses (Sezer, Gino, Norton).
- Gottman's relationship ratio of 5 positive to 1 negative interaction, adapted to work as at least 3:1, with researchers suggesting ~80 percent of feedback should be positive.
- Daniel Simons' 1997 perception video: managers usually fail to notice that the 'employee' is swapped for a different person until the third, fourth, or fifth viewing, proving how poorly we perceive others.
- Antonakis charisma training: a 5-hour intervention moved 34 Swiss managers from 2.45 to 3.00 on a 5-point charisma scale, with employees blind to the training rating 65 vs 35 percent better-than-average.
- Best leadership perception occurred when the leader's IQ was exactly 18 points above employees'; too large a gap (the curvilinear effect) reduced perceived effectiveness (Antonakis, 379 executives, average IQ 111).
- Mercedes F1 under Toto Wolff: introduced mindfulness (first 20 hostile engineers, now 1,000-plus) and an open error culture, winning eight consecutive titles 2014-2021.
- Zappos' humility test: a recruiter asks the shuttle-bus driver how the candidate treated them; mistreating the driver means no hire 'no matter how well the interviews went' (Tony Hsieh).
- Dweck mindset intervention lifted class-goal attainment by 10 percent overall and 20 percent in mathematics from reading about brain neuroplasticity.
- Steve Jobs to Corning CEO Wendell Weeks on gorilla glass: 'Don't be afraid. Yes, you can do it.' Corning's Harrisburg, Kentucky plant converted overnight and 'made a glass that had never been made' in under six months.
- Grant's task-significance fundraising study: reading scholarship-recipient stories lifted weekly funds raised from ~1,300 to over 3,100 dollars.
- Nelson Mandela persuading protocol chief John Reinders to stay: 'We people, we are from the bush. We do not know how to administer a body as complex as the presidency. We need experienced people such as yourself.'
- Benjamin Franklin: 'even if I could conceive that I had completely overcome it, I should probably be proud of my humility.'
- Owen's Hubris Syndrome 14 criteria, with 5 unique to hubris (items 5, 6, 10, 12, 13: conflating self with nation, using the royal 'we', unshakeable belief in vindication by history/God, restlessness/recklessness, moral rectitude overriding practicality); cited examples include Chamberlain, Mao, G.W. Bush, Thatcher, Blair, Trump, Bolsonaro.
- Edward Hess signing emails as lowercase 'ed' not 'Ed' as a daily quieting-ego reminder ('I am little ed, not big Ed').
- Source identity: ISBN 978-1-032-41420-1 hbk and 978-1-003-35801-5 ebk, DOI 10.4324/9781003358015, Routledge 2023, originally German 'Mit Demut zum Erfolg' (Springer 2022); author Franziska Frank, ESMT Berlin, ex-BCG, with an endorsement from Adam Grant.

## Curriculum fit
This book sits squarely in the leadership and creativity legs of the reading list and connects usefully to agentic-AI research, though it has nothing on security. For leadership, it is an unusually evidence-dense treatment: every claim is tied to a study or to 160-plus structured executive interviews and a 10-country survey, which models the same verification-over-assertion discipline that good security research demands (a scan produces candidates, a finding requires verified data; here a felt impression of a 'humble' leader is repeatedly tested against measured behavior and refuted when it is just self-flattery). For creativity, the chapters on constraints driving innovation (frugal innovation, Green Eggs and Ham, Renault's budget car), on awe and the small-self, and on psychological safety as the substrate for idea generation (Pixar Braintrust, Project Aristotle) are directly transferable to how one structures generative work. For agentic AI, three threads matter: the Pygmalion/golem dynamic (a supervisor's expectations subconsciously shaping a subordinate's output) is a sharp analogy for how an orchestrator's framing and prompts shape sub-agent performance, echoing the 'intelligence at input' principle; the feedback architectures (BET, BEAR, 4A, the curse-of-knowledge trap where experts underestimate what novices lack) map onto designing evaluation and task-spec loops for multi-agent systems; and the explicit treatment of perception failure, attribution error, and over-trust offers a vocabulary for thinking about where humans and orchestrators misjudge agent reliability. The book's weakness for this curriculum is that it is practitioner-facing and somewhat repetitive, and its 'everything can be learned' optimism is asserted more strongly than the cited studies always warrant. Net: a strong leadership and creativity entry, a moderate agentic-AI analogy source, and a good counterweight to ego-driven or purely technical leadership models.
