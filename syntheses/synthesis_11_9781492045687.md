# The Art of Leadership: Small Things, Done Well

ISBN: 9781492045687  |  Book #11

## One-liner
A field manual of 30 small, repeatable leadership habits, narrated as war stories across three career stages, arguing that leadership cannot be hacked but must be patiently built from practices compounded over years.

## Thesis
Lopp (the blogger "Rands") argues that effective leadership is not a set of hacks or a promotion you are equipped for, but a craft built from small things (concrete, repeatable practices) performed every working day until the compounding value of thousands of repetitions produces judgment. He structures the practices by career stage (manager, director, executive) and insists the through-line of every practice is trust, with the final and overarching principle being to be unfailingly kind. The closing test asks the reader to write their own leadership principles and self-score on Vision, Strategy, Tactics + Judgment (VST+J), then pick a small thing and practice it for three months.

Then use Judgment to pick the small things to practice for three months. Closing: there are no hacks, no silver bullets; you reach the summit by taking the next small step. Author bio and colophon.

## Core ideas
- You cannot hack leadership; it is built from small things repeated until they become judgment. The 1:1 example: the habit did not teach him 1:1s matter, the compounding value of thousands of them did.
- Management is not a promotion, it is a career restart. The IC instincts that earned the role actively steer the new manager wrong (the New Manager Death Spiral).
- Trust is the single binding agent under every practice. Delegation, diverse teams, feedback, and visibly letting others change your mind are all trust-construction mechanisms.
- Accountability is not the same as responsibility. Responsibility makes a manager say 'I' a lot and erode trust; accountability is the obligation to justify (give account for) actions and decisions, which your team has already equipped you to do by telling you things voluntarily.
- A delivered B can be credible leadership. Letting a team ship a B they own builds more long-term capability and trust than the A you could have produced solo by micromanaging.
- Culture is already mostly built by the founding Old Guard and will not significantly change while they run the company; culture lives in the stories people retell, not the values painted on the wall.
- In the absence of information, humans fill the gap with the worst possible version of the truth. This single rule explains rumors, the need to share meeting notes company-wide, and cascading communication plans.
- Fires burn faster uphill: the further up the org chart, the more fuel; most situations that reach an executive are pre-qualified five-alarm fires, so the executive's primary job is fire prevention, not firefighting.
- Distinguish the Zone (real creative building) from the Faux-Zone (the addictive chemical rush of busy that produces no creative value). Protect a precious hour every day to actually build.
- Leadership comes from everywhere. Build equal, parallel IC and manager career paths plus real non-manager leadership roles, or you signal that only managers lead, which creates hierarchy, silos, and politics.
- Spidey-sense (instant, experience-fed intuition) is real-time wisdom worth trusting and even operationalizing (the Spidey-Sense spreadsheet), distinct from fear-driven paranoia.
- Recruiting is the highest-leverage work a hiring manager does; commit one hour/day per open req up to 50% of your time, and treat candidates by mindset (Discover, Understand, Delight), not just funnel stage.
- Saying the hard thing and hearing the hard thing are paired skills; the goal in life is to make feedback in all directions 'no big deal,' which only happens after trust is built over months.
- Be unfailingly kind is the defining principle, modeled on DJ the Destiny raid leader: clear repeated explanation, expert answers, real-time educational feedback, never losing composure, every single time.
- At any point, ~30% of your team is unhappy with your performance simply because you are the leader; it is not personal, so do not run witch hunts over rumors, mine them for the real question being asked.

## Techniques and methods
- The morning calendar scrub: for each meeting precache 'what do I need to do to be prepared,' then subjectively rate how much value each meeting will create.
- The 8-question Situation decision workflow (am I the right person; do I have complete context; track record of sources; inconsistencies; can I explain multiple perspectives neutrally; my biases; my emotional state; and again, am I the right person).
- Act Last / Read the Room / Taste the Soup: like the poker button, speak last to gather context; open a talk by asking for a show of hands, a 1:1 with 'How are you?', and sample (not dictate) a project with small critical questions.
- The Spidey-Sense spreadsheet: a shared list where the team logs anyone who seems 'off,' reasons optional, reviewed weekly to surface early attrition/burnout patterns.
- The Professional Growth Questionnaire: ~19 written questions (strengths, last promotion, fair compensation, mentor, last 360, dream job, admired leaders) revisited 4-5 times a year, not just at performance season.
- The performance checklist sentence: 'multiple face-to-face conversations over multiple months where you clearly explained and agreed there is a gap and agreed to specific measurable actions' (minimum three substantive face-to-face conversations).
- Rands Information Practices: delete all bookmarks and rebuild from memory; pin <=5 browser tools; ad blocker; one browser window / <=10 tabs; inbox-zero scrubbing protocol; flag <=7 VIPs; delete unused apps; learn keyboard shortcuts.
- The Blue Tape List: in a new role, list everything that feels off, wait about a month, then address every item (fix it or explain why not).
- The leadership career-path delegation ladder (Manager -> Sr Manager -> Director -> Sr Director -> Executive), scaling on oversight, project magnitude/complexity, and team size.
- The recruiting model: 1 hour/day per open req capped at 50%; the Must List spreadsheet (everyone you must work with again, reviewed monthly, ~42 names); Discover/Understand/Delight; the Real Offer Letter sent a week before start.
- The three-point staff-meeting agenda (Minimal Metrics Story, Rolling Team-Sourced Topics, Gossip/Rumors/Lies) plus Meeting Runner and Meeting Historian roles and company-wide note sharing.
- The compliment definition and breakdown: a selfless, well-articulated, timely recognition of achievement (avoid 'doghouse roses' compliments with a cost attached).
- Hearing hard feedback: Step 1 listen for one glimpse of understanding (adopt the 'I am listening' posture); Step 2 repeat back what you heard to confirm and clarify.
- Rule of 3 and 10 (everything breaks at 3, 10, 30, 100 people) plus Five Whys root-cause process applied company-wide, not just to product bugs.
- The Org Chart Legibility Test: draw the org chart with a coworker, no human names, no excuses, and check a tenured employee would say 'yup, that's about right.'
- Distributed-meeting primer: Pre/During/Post checklist (schedule at X:05, arrive X:00; mute by default; assign a spotter; room with most people disconnects last; send decaying notes fast).
- Kobayashi prevention via cascading communications plan: frame in a written artifact; vet with three no-skin-in-the-game trusted humans; size the affected-people list; then preflight 1:1s -> persons-of-interest groups -> affected teams -> whole-org announcement.
- The Signal Network grid: plot information on criticality (vertical) x freshness (horizontal) into four quadrants (Stale Slowness, Voluminous Spam, Critically Fresh, the dangerous Important Slow).
- A Precious Hour: block one hour every day (including weekends) to build a thing, no to-do list, phone away, door closed.
- The 360 review process (hire a neutral consultant; supply a list of reports/peers/manager; same questions to all; anonymized aggregated 'doing well' vs 'needs help' columns) leading to finding a mentor.
- The How to Rands user guide format: Average Week, North Star Principles, Feedback Protocol, Meeting Protocol, Nuance and Errata, given to new reports to accelerate the relationship.
- The VST+J test: write your own leadership principles (30 min, revisit daily for a week); on a 3x2 grid (Vision/Strategy/Tactics rows x Current/Ideal columns) allocate 100 points per column; have a trusted person rank you and compare; then use Judgment to pick small things to practice for three months.

## Curriculum fit
This is the human-systems / leadership leg of the reading list, and it pairs directly with the orchestration and agentic-AI work. Lopp treats teams as systems to debug ('I see things as systems. I think in flowcharts'), which maps cleanly onto designing multi-agent pipelines, subagent task specs, and the orchestrator-plus-delegation pattern: his delegation ladder (scaling on oversight, magnitude, and team composition) is the same calculus as deciding which tier of agent gets which scoped task, and his 'a delivered B that the team owns beats the A you micromanaged' is a direct argument for trusting Haiku/Sonnet executors fed rich upstream context rather than redoing their work. The Signal Network criticality-x-freshness grid is a usable lens for triaging findings/alerts in a research program, and his 'verification of the hard thing' and 'mine the rumor for the real question' discipline rhymes with the NuClide verify-before-asserting and codify-every-survey ethics. For the creativity strand, Anti-Flow (deliberately engineering unstructured time for inspiration) and the Precious Hour are concrete anti-busy practices. For leadership specifically, the VST+J self-assessment and the 'write your own principles' exercise are immediately actionable. Caveat for this reader: it is engineering-management memoir, not security or AI-infra content, so its value is methodological and human-facing rather than technical; the stories are admittedly fictionalized, so treat the numbers (14% fixed, 75% Asch, ~42 Must List names) as illustrative anecdotes, not citable data.
