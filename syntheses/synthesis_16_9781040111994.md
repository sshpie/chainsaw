# Psybersecurity: Human Factors of Cyber Defence

ISBN: 9781040111994  |  Book #16

## One-liner
An Edith Cowan University edited volume arguing that the human mind is the real attack surface of cyber defence, coining "psybersecurity" (the mental-health attack surface) and surveying culture, leadership, GenAI harms, education, and cyber-range training that follow from putting people, not technology, at the centre.

## Thesis
The book's central claim is that cybersecurity is fundamentally a human-factors discipline: with over 90% of cyberattacks (WEF 2022) exploiting human vulnerabilities, the people, their psychology, culture, and behaviour are both the weakest link and the strongest potential defence. It coins "psybersecurity" to name an emerging, under-defended attack surface where cyberattacks deliberately target mental health and cognition, and extends the human lens across cyber-physical systems, social-engineering-driven trust erosion, generative-AI weaponisation, organisational security culture/leadership, K-12 education, and cyber-range training. The unifying agenda is Australia's national goal to become the most cyber-secure nation by 2030, framing human competence and resilience as the keystone of that effort. Across nine chapters the editors argue the field must shift from prevention-centric, technology-first, gatekeeping postures toward enablement, lived ethics, behavioural change, and psychological readiness.

## Structure
### Front matter and framing (Guidetti, Ahmed, Speelman, editors; CRC Press 2025)
Edited volume mostly from Edith Cowan University, Joondalup, Western Australia. Sets the human-factors thesis: WEF 2022 Global Risks Report says >90% of cyberattacks exploit human vulnerabilities; anchors the book to Australia's 2023-2030 strategy goal of being the most cyber-secure nation by 2030. DOI 10.1201/9781032664859; nine chapters.

### Ch 1-2: Systemic resilience and cyber-physical / OT systems
Frames cybersecurity culture and systemic resilience against the NIST CSF (identify/protect/detect/respond/recover) and an Integrated Cybersecurity Framework. Covers cyber-physical systems, ICS/SCADA, HMI, safety instrumented systems, IIoT and Industry 4.0; argues OT incidents carry physical-safety and mental-health consequences (WannaCry, equipment damage) that purely technical models miss.

### Ch 3-4: Social engineering, digitisation of self, and psybersecurity (the coined field)
Examines social engineering, phishing/vishing, impersonation, synthetic and stolen identity, and the digitisation of personal data as inference surface. Introduces psybersecurity: cyberattacks that target mental health (anxiety, depression, fear) as the objective, not just the vector. Builds a Psybersecurity Risk Framework adapting Parasuraman & Riley's use/misuse/disuse/abuse automation model to the human-as-target.

### Ch 5-6: Generative AI harms, deepfakes, and governance
Covers GANs (generator + discriminator), VAEs/autoencoders, deepfakes, voice cloning, WormGPT, misinformation, harassment, non-consensual imagery, and synthetic-identity fraud. Maps the governance response: EU AI Act, GDPR, OECD AI principles, explainable AI (XAI) against the black-box effect, and blockchain for AI accountability. Stresses jurisdictional gaps and the asymmetry between fast harms and slow law.

### Ch 7: Security through influence over mandate (Armitage & Guidetti) - the leadership chapter
Five security-culture patterns for change: (1) security is everyone's job / make it tangible; (2) security and productivity are not opposed (product/customer-centric security); (3) manage resistance (Nemawashi, Wardley explorers/villagers/town planners, navigating bureaucracy, 'in tension' metrics); (4) security through continuous small steps (Flow Framework, Theory of Constraints five focusing steps, hyperbolic discounting); (5) security is enablement not gatekeeping (escape 'The Church of No' via Cynefin-classified guardrails, threat modelling, Elevation of Privilege).

### Ch 8: Human skills and sociocultural impacts of K-12 cybersecurity education (Sikos, McKee, Ibrahim, Johnson)
Argues cybersecurity must be taught from primary school as a 'lived ethic', balancing behavioural and technical competencies. Uses the DQ Institute / IEEE 3527.1-2020 digital-intelligence framework (8 areas, 24 competencies), the KAB (knowledge-attitude-behaviour) model and HAIS-Q, cyber-wisdom, cyber-hygiene, and habit-over-vigilance. Critiques adult-centric frameworks that ignore children, the media 'hoodie in the dark' stereotype, and the workforce shortage.

### Ch 9: Incident-response drills on cyber ranges (Grojek, Sikos, Holmes, Guidetti)
Positions cyber ranges (DARPA National Cyber Range lineage; CyberBit, Cyber-MAR, CRaaS) as the bridge from theory to practice, distinguishing education from training and emphasising psychological readiness/resilience. Draws a sustained parallel to aviation flight simulators (training transfer, fidelity regulation, CPD) and gives a cognitive-complexity formula (Eq 9.1) plus the SOCI Act critical-asset taxonomy and red/blue/white/green team structure.

### Back matter: per-chapter references and Index
Each chapter carries a dense academic reference list (frameworks, primary studies, books such as Team Topologies, Software Engineering at Google, The Checklist Manifesto). The Index (FILE 19/19) catalogues the human-factors vocabulary end to end, from 'abuse' and 'anxiety' through 'vigilance', 'WannaCry', and 'WormGPT'.

## Core ideas
- Psybersecurity is the book's coined field: an attack surface where the objective is the victim's mental health and cognition (inducing anxiety, fear, depression), not merely using psychology as a delivery vector. It is presented as under-recognised and under-defended.
- The human is simultaneously the weakest link and the first/strongest line of defence. The repeated line 'we are all our first line of defence' reframes every user, teacher, and child as a defensive node.
- Over 90% of cyberattacks exploit human vulnerabilities (WEF 2022 Global Risks Report). This single statistic is the load-bearing justification for the entire volume's human-factors pivot.
- Security culture beats security mandate. Durable change comes from influence, shared understanding of 'why', and lived ethics, not from imposed controls; resistance is a signal to be managed, not crushed (too much AND too little resistance are both problems).
- Security and productivity are not a zero-sum trade. Treating security as a product with internal customers, and measuring induced friction with 'in tension' metrics, dissolves the false dichotomy and the 'us versus them' divide.
- Security is enablement, not gatekeeping. The enterprise security 'Church of No' is untenable when teams deploy multiple times daily; security must scale sub-linearly through engineering, guardrails, and decentralised authority.
- Cynefin-based control classification: complicated controls (well-understood, expert-solvable) should become automated guardrails; complex controls (interdependent, uncertain) need adaptive methods like collaborative threat modelling.
- Behavioural change is the real objective of education, and it follows the KAB chain (knowledge raises attitude, which improves behaviour). For children, habit must precede vigilance because sustained vigilance is unsustainable even in trained security technicians.
- Cybersecurity should be taught as a 'lived ethic' grounded in universal moral values (privacy, freedom), not primarily as nation-protection. Both privacy and ethics are framed as the core of cybersecurity.
- Generative AI industrialises human-factors attacks: deepfakes, voice cloning, WormGPT, and synthetic identities scale deception, while governance (EU AI Act, GDPR, OECD principles, XAI, blockchain accountability) lags the harm and is fragmented by jurisdiction.
- Training is not education. Education gives principles and theory; training gives operational readiness under pressure. Cyber ranges and flight simulators both exist to produce 'training transfer' from safe simulation to real high-stakes performance.
- Aviation is the model discipline for cyber: a more mature, more regulated field where simulators are mandatory, fidelity is legally standardised, and continuing professional development (CPD) is enforced; cyber should adopt the same formalised CPD, especially for SOCI critical-asset operators.
- Cybersecurity's workforce problem is a diversity-of-people problem, not a diversity-of-technology problem. The field is industry-agnostic and spans psychology, education, communications, design, and law, so it needs broad human profiles, not just 'tech-heads'.
- Cyber-physical and OT exposure carries physical-safety and psychological stakes that IT-only threat models miss; systemic resilience must span identify-protect-detect-respond-recover across human, organisational, and machine layers.
- Cognitive load and human attention are finite and exploitable. Defender attention is a scarce resource (vigilance fatigue), and security designs that ignore cognitive load (curse-of-knowledge bias, desire paths) fail in practice.

## Techniques and methods
- Integrated Cybersecurity Framework (ICF) and NIST Cybersecurity Framework (identify / protect / detect / respond / recover) applied to systemic resilience and culture.
- Psybersecurity Risk Framework: adaptation of Parasuraman & Riley's use / misuse / disuse / abuse automation model to humans as the target of attack.
- DQ Institute Digital Intelligence framework (built on IEEE 3527.1-2020): 8 areas of digital life (literacy, use, safety, security, emotional intelligence, communication, rights, identity) and 24 competencies across citizenship / creativity / competitiveness levels.
- KAB learning model (Knowledge - Attitude - Behaviour) operationalised via the Human Aspects of Information Security Questionnaire (HAIS-Q, Parsons et al. 2017).
- Cyber-wisdom four-component model: cyber-wisdom literacy, reasoning, self-reflection, and motivation (Polizzi & Harrison 2022).
- Ch 7 five security-culture patterns, each with a named toolkit: security-is-job-zero/tangible; product-and-customer-centric security; resistance management (Nemawashi, Wardley explorers-villagers-town-planners, 'in tension' metrics, Schwartz's Monkey/Razor/Sumo-Wrestler bureaucracy strategies); continuous small steps (Flow Framework feature/defect/debt/risk distribution, Theory of Constraints five focusing steps, 'weeding and feeding'); enablement (Cynefin complex-vs-complicated, Team Topologies collaboration/facilitation/as-a-service, threat modelling, Elevation of Privilege card game).
- Theory of Constraints five focusing steps: identify, optimise (exploit), subordinate, elevate, iterate (Goldratt & Cox; TameFlow / Tendon 2022).
- Security Chaos Engineering as a method to test resilience by deliberately injecting failure (Rinehart & Shortridge, derived from Netflix chaos engineering).
- Threat modelling as collaborative defence design (Shostack 2014), gamified via the Elevation of Privilege card game to turn security into a playful 'enabling constraint'.
- Cyber-range training architecture: simulation / emulation / overlay / hybrid range types; green/white/red/blue team structure; Cyber Range Design Framework (Katsantonis et al. 2023); distributed trainee-monitoring architecture (Andreolini et al. 2020) measuring speed-to-objective and action correctness; wearable sensors to capture participant pressure.
- Equation 9.1 - cognitive complexity C(O) of a cyber-range operating environment modelled as the weighted arithmetic mean of quantified properties p1..n with weights w1..n (low/medium/high impact); explicitly notes task performance cannot directly measure this complexity.
- Aviation training-transfer method: A/B comparison of airborne-only vs simulator-trained cohorts against clear measurable competency criteria (Allerton & Ross 1991; Baldwin & Ford 1988), offloading high-risk practice (e.g. ~25 hours of low-visibility instrument approach) into the simulator.
- Capture-the-flag (CTF) jeopardy-style competitions with students alternating offensive/defensive roles, and VM-based formative assessment, as pedagogy inside ranges.

## Memorable
- 'Psybersecurity' - the book's coined term for the mental-health attack surface; the Index ends, fittingly, on 'WormGPT, 98'.
- WEF 2022 Global Risks Report: over 90% of cyberattacks exploit human vulnerabilities.
- Australia's stated goal: become the most cyber-secure nation by 2030 (2023-2030 Australian Cyber Security Strategy), used as the book's national frame.
- 'The Church of No' - the label for legacy enterprise security as a gatekeeper on the path to production; contrasted with AWS's 'Security is job zero' (Rodgers 2018).
- 'Hope is not a strategy' (Murphy et al. / Google SRE 2016) and Parkinson's Law - 'work expands to fill the time allotted for its completion' (1955) - both invoked for continuous-small-steps security.
- Learning-retention figures cited: roughly 10% from reading, 75% from practice, 90% from teaching others (Hallam et al. 1955).
- Log4Shell (log4j 0-day, Wortley et al. 2021) used as the worked example: Google's shared-empowerment model patched with minimal coordination versus the protracted multi-team scramble in traditional enterprises.
- Training-transfer attrition stats (Ch 9): ~40% of trainees fail to transfer skills immediately (Saks 2002), ~70% falter after one year, and only ~50% of training investment yields real improvement; Georgenson (1982) put behavioural-change success as low as 10%.
- A B-757/767 civil flight simulator costs over $35 million with operating costs of thousands of dollars per hour (Lofaro & Smith 2023); first flight simulator dates to 1929.
- The 'curse of knowledge' bias (Camerer et al. 1989) and 'desire paths' (Tomes 2022) as why experts' obvious controls get bypassed; Princess Leia's 'the more you tighten your grip...' quoted on over-control.
- Cyberbullying is 'nothing but the digital manifestation of traditional bullying' (Olweus 2012); leaving children's passwords on the classroom wall is called 'a cybersecurity nightmare'.
- Named tools/platforms: DARPA National Cyber Range, CyberBit Range, Cyber-MAR (maritime), Cyber Range as a Service (CRaaS), VMware/Hyper-V; the SOCI Act 2018 critical-asset register (11 sectors incl. energy, healthcare, financial, electoral, maritime ports).
- Vigilance appears in 43% of K-12 cybersecurity-education research papers (Ibrahim et al.), yet the authors argue vigilance is 'overkill' for children and unsustainable even in security technicians - 'habit precedes vigilance'.
- Schwartz's bureaucracy toolkit: the Monkey, the Razor, and the Sumo Wrestler (The Delicate Art of Bureaucracy, 2020); Wardley's explorers / villagers / town planners for engaging the right people at the right time.
- 'Security is an infinite game; success is continually being able to keep playing.'

## Curriculum fit
This book is a strong human-layer complement to a security-research + creativity + leadership + agentic-AI reading list, and earns its place precisely where pure technical exploitation literature goes silent. For security research, it reframes the attack surface as cognitive and organisational, which directly informs social-engineering chains, OT/ICS human-machine failure modes, and the psybersecurity angle of impact (the mental-health and trust consequences a CISO actually fears) - useful for the 'make the CISO feel the risk' narrative-reporting standard. For leadership, Chapter 7 is the standout: it is essentially a field manual for driving security change through influence rather than mandate (Nemawashi, Wardley, Cynefin guardrails, Theory of Constraints, 'security is enablement not gatekeeping'), mapping cleanly onto orchestrator/defender-empathy thinking and the move from 'Church of No' to a product-minded security function. For creativity, the gamified threat-modelling (Elevation of Privilege), security chaos engineering, desire-paths/curse-of-knowledge framing, and the deepfake/GenAI-harms chapter feed adversarial-imagination and red-team scenario design. For agentic-AI research, Chapters 5-6 and 9 are most relevant: GANs/VAEs/WormGPT as offensive primitives, XAI versus the black-box effect, EU AI Act/GDPR/OECD governance, and AI/ML-driven adaptive cyber ranges - plus the aviation-simulator 'training transfer' lens, which is a clean analogue for evaluating and certifying autonomous agents under realistic, repeatable, safe-but-pressured conditions. Weaknesses to flag for the curriculum: it is an Australia-centric, education-and-culture-heavy edited volume with uneven depth, light on concrete CVEs/exploit primitives and on rigorous metrics, so it should be read as the human/organisational/governance counterweight to hands-on offensive texts rather than a source of working primitives.
