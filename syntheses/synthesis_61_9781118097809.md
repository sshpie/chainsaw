# Book 61: Computational Intelligence and Pattern Analysis in Biological Informatics

We read this as the bridge book between our optimization lobe and our biology lobe. It is a 2010 Wiley volume edited by Maulik, Bandyopadhyay, and Wang, sixteen contributed chapters in five parts, and its single argument is simple: real biological questions are pattern-recognition problems, and soft computing is the toolbox that turns them into solvable ones. Where our other bio books lean on exact algorithms or wet-lab method, this one leans on tolerance of imprecision. Fuzzy sets, neural nets, and population-based search are not decoration here; each is wired into a concrete pipeline with a named dataset and a validity number.

The spine is Part I. Chapter 1 is a compressed catalog of the whole soft-computing arsenal we care about: fuzzy logic with its T-norms and defuzzification rules, GA and its descendants DE, PSO (pbest/gbest, C1/C2), ACO (stigmergy), and BFOA (chemotaxis and swarming), rough sets with Pawlak approximations, and Pearl belief networks. Chapter 2 is the pattern-recognition half: feature-selection distances (Bhattacharyya, Mahalanobis, divergence), branch-and-bound, filter versus wrapper, PCA and the K-L transform, k-NN, Bayes, and the classifier zoo (MLP, RBF, SVM, SOM). Everything downstream is an application of these two chapters, so we treat them as the reusable core and the rest as worked examples.

The examples are the value. On sequence, we get n-gram plus Bior3.3-wavelet promoter recognition with cascaded neural voting, and miRanda-style Smith-Waterman miRNA target scoring. On structure, we get the ideas we reach for most: molecule-as-graph frequent-subgraph mining (gSpan, GASTON), optimal-assignment kernels via the Hungarian algorithm, pattern-diffusion kernels, SCOP neighborhood and spectral protein kernels, and Protein Blocks (sixteen SOM-derived pentapeptide prototypes at roughly 0.42 A RMSD). Chapter 10's variable-length GA for de novo ligand design is a clean primitive: a 41-fragment tree chromosome, a Lennard-Jones-plus-electrostatic fitness, adaptive Srinivas-Patnaik operators, docked against HIV-1 protease (1AAQ, RMSD 1.97 A to Ritonavir) and thrombin (1DWD).

Part IV is our clustering reference. The recurring lesson is that clustering quality is never self-evident; it is scored by Jm, the Maulik-Bandyopadhyay I-index, silhouette s(C), and Xie-Beni, then confirmed by GO enrichment at p<0.01. Metaheuristic clustering (DEFC, CR=0.7, F=0.8) beats local FCM, and bolting a proximity-trained SVM onto the clusters lifts s(C) further because supervised beats unsupervised once labels are trusted. Chapter 13's ParPSBKM shows the same clustering run in MPI/C reaching 8x speedup, which is exactly where our parallel-compute lobe plugs in. Chapter 12's four-kernel SVM ensemble plus SNR and NSGA-II selection collapses 100 genes to eleven markers, our template for multiobjective feature reduction.

Part V is systems biology and the strongest cross-lobe pull. Disease-gene prioritization moves from text-mining profile vectors through local PPI counting to global network distance, and the honest finding is that random-walk-with-restart and diffusion kernels win (RWR mean enrichment 26 versus 18 for shortest-path). Chapter 16 mines dense k-subnetworks from the scale-free, sparse human interactome (HPRD, clustering coefficient 1.13E-4), using quasi-cliques and Czekanowski-Dice neighborhood weighting because strict cliques break on noisy data.

We keep two cautions. First, the book is dated: tool names and database versions are historical, and the text mangles most equations, so we mine it for method and parameters, not formulas. Second, it is a survey of self-benchmarked papers, so its winner claims are leads to verify, not settled results. Read alongside books 56, 57, and 62, it is the connective tissue that maps our optimizers onto biology.
