# 58. Biological Computation

## Biological Computation (Book 58, bio-compute)

We read this as the map of a lobe, not a single tool. Lamm and Unger take four ways biology reshaped computing and reduce each to its named algorithm, its governing equation, and the exact point where it fails. That third thing is why we keep it.

### The through-line

Every model in the book is the same bet: a large population of simple, local, parallel units, no central control, and behavior that emerges from the interaction rather than the parts. Conway's Game of Life reaches Turing-completeness from two rules (birth on 3 neighbors, survive on 2 or 3). Genetic algorithms search a space no gradient can, on fixed-length chromosomes with crossover and mutation. DNA computing runs generate-and-test at Avogadro scale. Ant colonies route packets. Once we see the pattern, we stop treating emergence as a buzzword and start treating it as an engineering choice with known costs.

### What earns its place for us

Three threads are directly load-bearing. First, evolutionary search is the theory under evolutionary fuzzing. Holland's schema theorem, the building-block hypothesis, and the operator taxonomy (roulette, rank, tournament, elitism selection, pc crossover, pm mutation, inversion) are exactly the knobs a coverage-guided fuzzer exposes. When we reason about why AFL-style mutation finds crashes, this is the vocabulary.

Second, the artificial immune system is a self/nonself anomaly detector we can actually reason about. LISYS encodes every connection as a 49-bit IP/IP/port tuple, generates random detectors, deletes any that match self during a tolerization window, and fires on r-contiguous-bit matches (r=12, tau=10). It is a 1990s design, but negative selection is a clean mental model for behavioral detection, and the co-stimulation step (a human confirms within Ts) is an honest answer to false-positive fatigue.

Third, molecular computation is a calibration exercise. Adleman solved a 7-vertex Hamiltonian path in DNA, Lipton did SAT, Shapiro built a finite automaton out of FokI cleavage and sticky ends. Impressive, and the book is disciplined about the ceiling: molecule count grows as d^N, an 80-vertex instance wants 10^80 molecules, and the authors name the two real obstacles (per-problem design cost, physical error rate). We carry that restraint into any DNA-breaks-crypto claim.

### How we route it

The neural chapter (perceptron limits, backprop, Hopfield capacity near 0.138N, self-organizing maps) is foundation, not frontier; we pair it with book 24 (Securing AI Systems) and book 61 for anything modern. The systems-biology close (Kitano's four robustness mechanisms, Simon's near-decomposable Hora and Tempus watchmakers, 82% of yeast genes individually dispensable) is the biological mirror of book 33 (Chaos Engineering): redundancy, modularity, negative feedback, graceful degradation. The whole inherent-parallelism argument only cashes out on real hardware, which is where book 59 (Programming Massively Parallel Processors) takes over.

### The honest limit

This is a 2011 teaching survey. It gives us the concepts, the math, and the failure modes at survey depth, then points at primary papers. For engineering-grade metaheuristics we go to books 62, 64, and 65; for sequence work, book 55. We use 58 to prime the thinking and to keep our claims proportionate, which is exactly what a lobe map is for.
